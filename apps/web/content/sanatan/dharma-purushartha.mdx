The Sanatan tradition speaks of four legitimate aims of human life — the *purusharthas* (पुरुषार्थ): *dharma*, *artha*, *kama*, and *moksha*. Of these, dharma is listed first, not as one option among four but as the orienting aim that gives the other three their proper shape and limit.

## Dharma as the First Aim

The Mahabharata and the Dharmashastras emphasise that artha (wealth) and kama (pleasure) are good only when pursued *dharmena* — by dharmic means. The Kamasutra itself, often misread, opens by acknowledging dharma's priority: kama unmoored from dharma corrodes the very life it claims to enrich. Manusmriti 2.224 places dharma at the top of any rightly ordered life.

## Samanya and Vishesha Dharma

Dharma in this context has two faces. *Samanya dharma* names the duties common to all human beings — truth, non-injury, restraint, purity, compassion, generosity. *Vishesha dharma* names the particular duties attaching to one's stage of life (*ashrama*), social role, family obligations, and circumstances. Living well requires attention to both.

## Svadharma

The Bhagavad Gita's contribution to the purushartha tradition is its insistence on *svadharma* — one's own situated dharma. Krishna tells Arjuna *śreyān svadharmo viguṇaḥ paradharmāt svanuṣṭhitāt* (3.35) — better one's own dharma imperfectly performed than another's well performed. Discerning one's svadharma in changing circumstances is itself a spiritual discipline.

## Dharma and the Other Aims

Dharma is not the enemy of artha and kama; it is their architect. Earned by dharmic labour, wealth supports family, community, and yajna. Pursued within dharma, pleasure deepens relationship rather than exhausting it. The classical Sanatan vision is not ascetic suspicion of the world but ordered participation in it.

## Dharma and Moksha

The relation to moksha is more layered. The pursuit of dharma purifies the heart and prepares it for liberation. Yet at the highest stage, even dharma must be transcended — not violated, but seen through. The Gita's *sarva-dharmān parityajya* (18.66) does not abolish dharma; it relocates the self to the source of dharma itself.

## A Living Compass

Lived as a daily orientation, dharma asks at each fork: is this choice upholding what should be upheld? Is it serving the larger weave, or only my own short-term wish? Answered patiently, this question keeps the four purusharthas in balance and a human life rightly aimed.
