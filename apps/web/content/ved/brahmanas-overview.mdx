The **Brāhmaṇas** (ब्राह्मण) constitute the second layer of the Vedic textual corpus, attached to each of the four Vedas immediately after the Samhitas. They are large prose works whose principal subject is the ritual: the meaning of each rite, the proper performance of each act, the cosmological correspondences that underlie the actions, and the consequences — both immediate and post-mortem — of correct and incorrect performance. The Brahmanas are simultaneously theological commentary, ritual handbook, and the earliest sustained Sanskrit prose literature in the tradition.

## What "Brahmana" Means

The term *brāhmaṇa* in this context refers not to the priestly class (though that meaning is related) but to a body of teaching *concerning brahman* — here understood in its older sense of "sacred utterance" or "ritual power." A Brahmana text is a discourse on the inner meaning of the *brahman* of the Samhita. The Brahmanas explain *why* the mantras are uttered, *what* cosmic effect they produce, and *how* the ritual gestures embody and enact those effects.

## The Principal Brahmanas

Each Veda has at least one major Brahmana attached to it:

### Rigveda

- The **Aitareya Brāhmaṇa**, attached to the Shakala school of the Rigveda — particularly concerned with the *somayāga* and the royal consecration.
- The **Kauṣītaki Brāhmaṇa** (also called the Śāṅkhāyana Brāhmaṇa), a shorter parallel.

### Krishna Yajurveda

- The **Taittirīya Brāhmaṇa**, used widely in south India — a continuation of the Taittirīya Samhita in style and content.

### Shukla Yajurveda

- The **Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa** — "the Brahmana of a Hundred Paths" — by far the largest single Brahmana and one of the longest prose texts in Sanskrit. Composed in 100 *adhyāyas* (whence the name) divided across 14 *kāṇḍas*, it covers virtually every major Vedic ritual at length and contains many of the most famous mythological passages of the Vedic period, including the story of the great flood and Manu, the cycle of Yajnavalkya, and the cosmogony from the cosmic egg.

### Samaveda

- The **Tāṇḍya** (or Pañcaviṃśa) **Brāhmaṇa** — "the Brahmana of Twenty-Five" sections.
- The **Jaiminīya Brāhmaṇa** — preserved primarily in south India and famous for its many mythological narratives.

### Atharvaveda

- The **Gopatha Brāhmaṇa**, the sole surviving Brahmana of the Atharvaveda.

## Style and Method

The Brahmanas have a distinctive prose style — argumentative, expository, often discursive. A typical passage proceeds by stating a ritual action, asking why it is so, offering a cosmological or mythological explanation, citing a Vedic mantra, drawing a moral, and moving on. The argumentation is associative rather than rigorous; the Brahmana mind moves by *bandhu* — by analogical connection — finding correspondences (*upaniṣad*, in the older sense of "underlying connection") between the cosmic, the bodily, and the ritual.

A characteristic move is the identification: *X is Y*. The Vedic priest is identified with Prajapati; the altar with the cosmos; the year with the ritual; the fire with the sun; the breaths with the cardinal directions. By making these identifications, the priest performs cosmically; what is done at the altar is what happens in the universe.

## Major Themes

Several themes dominate the Brahmana literature:

- **The cosmic centrality of yajña.** The sacrifice is held to maintain the cosmos itself. The gods perform sacrifices to maintain their immortality; the rains, the sun, the seasons all depend on the proper performance of rites.
- **The figure of Prajāpati.** Where the Rigveda's principal cosmic agent is Indra, the Brahmanas elevate **Prajāpati** (the Lord of Creatures) to the central position. Many Brahmana passages are essentially Prajapati theology — describing how he created the worlds, exhausted himself in doing so, and is restored by the offerings of his creatures.
- **Cosmological speculation.** The Brahmanas develop early theories of cosmic time, of post-mortem destiny, of the relationship between man and the universe. They contain the earliest articulation of the doctrine that good and bad actions accumulate consequences — a doctrine that the Upanishads will refine into the mature theory of *karma*.
- **The two paths after death.** The doctrine of the *devayāna* (path of the gods) and the *pitṛyāna* (path of the ancestors) is articulated in the Brahmanas before being taken up by the early Upanishads.

## The Bridge to the Upanishads

The Brahmanas are not yet philosophical literature in the Upanishadic sense, but they are clearly moving in that direction. Within the Shatapatha and the Jaiminiya Brahmanas one already finds passages of clear meditative inquiry — sustained exploration of breath, of the meaning of OM, of the relation of the self to the cosmos — that the Aranyakas will deepen and the Upanishads will systematize. The earliest Upanishads (Brihadaranyaka and Chandogya) are physically continuous with the Brahmana texts to which they are attached; in the manuscript tradition the transition from Brahmana to Upanishad is often imperceptible.

## Scholarly and Religious Use

The Brahmanas were the working manuals of the Vedic priesthood — the texts from which a priest learned not just *what* to do but *why* it would work. As Vedic *śrauta* sacrifice declined in importance over the post-Vedic centuries, the Brahmanas lost their primary practical function. But they have remained the indispensable historical source for understanding how the Vedic ritual system was understood by its own practitioners — and the indispensable scriptural foundation, alongside the Samhitas, for the Mimamsa school of Vedic exegesis whose entire interpretive project rests on Brahmana-style analysis of ritual.
