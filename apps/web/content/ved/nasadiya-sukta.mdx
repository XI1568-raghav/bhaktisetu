The **Nāsadīya Sūkta** (नासदीय सूक्त), Rigveda 10.129, is among the most philosophically arresting hymns in world literature. In seven brief verses an unnamed Rishi confronts the question of cosmic origin and arrives, astonishingly, at a posture of disciplined uncertainty. The text is often called the "Hymn of Creation," but its true subject is the limit of human knowledge of creation.

## Opening Verses

The hymn begins with its title-phrase:

नासदासीन्नो सदासीत्तदानीं नासीद्रजो नो व्योमा परो यत् ।
*nāsad āsīn no sad āsīt tadānīṃ nāsīd rajo no vyomā paro yat*
"Then neither non-being existed nor being; there was no realm of air, no sky beyond."

The opening negation gives the hymn its name (*nāsad* = "not non-being"). In a single stroke the Rishi sets aside both the categories of *sat* (being) and *asat* (non-being) as inadequate to the primordial state. There was no death, no immortality, no day, no night. There was, the second verse adds, only "That One" (*tad ekam*), breathing without breath by its own power.

## Desire and the First Stirring

The third and fourth verses introduce two remarkable concepts. *Tapas* (तपस्, ascetic heat or creative intensity) generates the One into manifestation. Then *kāma* (काम, desire) arises — "the first seed of mind." The poet observes that the wise, searching in their hearts, discovered the bond between being and non-being as desire itself. This is one of the earliest psychological accounts of cosmogenesis in any tradition: creation begins not with a divine fiat but with a stirring of wanting.

## The Question of the Witnesses

Verse 6 turns to method:

को अद्धा वेद क इह प्र वोचत्कुत आजाता कुत इयं विसृष्टिः ।
*ko addhā veda ka iha pra vocat kuta ājātā kuta iyaṃ visṛṣṭiḥ*
"Who truly knows? Who here can declare it? Whence was it born, whence this creation?"

The gods themselves, the verse continues, are *posterior* to the creation — so even they cannot serve as witnesses. The implication is staggering: there is no authoritative source for cosmogonic knowledge, because every possible knower came after the event being described.

## The Final Verse

The hymn closes with a sentence of legendary ambiguity:

यो अस्याध्यक्षः परमे व्योमन्त्सो अङ्ग वेद यदि वा न वेद ॥
*yo asyādhyakṣaḥ parame vyoman so aṅga veda yadi vā na veda*
"He who is the overseer of this world in the highest heaven — he surely knows, or perhaps he knows not."

This *or perhaps he knows not* (*yadi vā na veda*) is among the most quoted phrases in Indian intellectual history. The hymn does not deny the existence of a supreme witness, but it refuses the easy assertion that such a witness possesses certain knowledge.

## Interpretive Traditions

Vedantic commentators read the Nasadiya as pointing to the inexpressible nature of *brahman* — beyond *sat* and *asat*, beyond the dualities of language. Skeptical readers, both ancient and modern, have seen it as evidence that the Vedic poets were comfortable holding open questions open. The German Indologist Max Müller called it "the noblest of Sanskrit hymns." Carl Sagan quoted it in *Cosmos* as an example of cosmological humility — the recognition that the universe outruns our certainty.

## Place in the Tradition

The Nasadiya Sukta belongs to Mandala 10, the latest layer of the Rigveda, where speculative hymns cluster. It marks the moment when the Vedic poetic mind, having sung the praises of the gods for centuries, turns to ask the more vertiginous question: *who or what stands behind the gods themselves?* That turn — from praise to inquiry — is arguably the seed from which the entire Upanishadic and Vedantic project will later grow.
