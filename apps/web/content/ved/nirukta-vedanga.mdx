**Nirukta** (निरुक्त) — literally "explanation" or "etymological derivation" — is the fourth of the six Vedangas. It is the discipline of explaining the obscure or archaic words of the Vedic hymns by tracing them to their etymological roots and uncovering their original meaning. The Vedic language by the time of the late Vedic period was already partly opaque to its inheritors: words from the older Mandalas had fallen out of common use, place-names and ritual terms had grown unfamiliar, and many passages required interpretation. Nirukta was the discipline that supplied this interpretation.

## The Nighantu

The Nirukta tradition begins with a much older work called the **Nighaṇṭu** (निघण्टु) — a collection of difficult Vedic words organized into thematic lists. The Nighantu has five sections (*adhyāyas*):

- **Naighaṇṭuka-kāṇḍa** — synonyms (words meaning earth, water, sky, etc.).
- **Naigama-kāṇḍa** — homonyms and ambiguous Vedic words.
- **Daivata-kāṇḍa** — names of deities, organized by cosmic level.

The Nighantu does not explain the words; it merely lists them. It is the *raw material* on which the Nirukta operates.

## Yaska's Nirukta

The foundational text of the discipline is the **Nirukta** of **Yāska** (probably 6th century BCE, predating Panini), composed in twelve books. Yaska takes the Nighantu as his data and offers etymological explanations for the listed words, often citing the Rigvedic verse in which the word occurs and proposing a derivation that illuminates the verse's meaning.

The Nirukta is the oldest surviving etymological treatise in the world. It also preserves invaluable references to earlier grammarians and to alternative interpretations of Vedic passages — debates that would otherwise be lost to us. Yaska's work is therefore a window onto a flourishing pre-Panini intellectual culture.

## Method

Yaska's etymological method has four characteristic features:

- **Root analysis.** Every noun, Yaska argues, derives from a verbal root (*dhātu*). Even names like *agni* are traced to roots — Yaska proposes several possible derivations and lets the reader choose.
- **Multiple derivations welcomed.** Where multiple plausible etymologies exist, Yaska often gives all of them, treating them as complementary rather than competing.
- **Phonetic flexibility.** Yaska permits considerable phonetic variation in etymological derivation — *r* may become *l*, vowels may shift, syllables may metathesize. Modern historical linguistics is much stricter; Yaska's method is closer to what we might call *creative etymology*.
- **Theological reading.** The derivation is offered not as antiquarian curiosity but as the key to the verse's spiritual or ritual meaning.

## A Famous Discussion — Nirukta 1.20

Yaska records a celebrated dispute. Some scholars hold that all nouns are derived from verbs; others (the school of Śākaṭāyana) hold that only some nouns are so derived, and that others are primitive. Yaska defends the first view, arguing that the very *function* of a noun is to designate an entity in some act of becoming — to be a noun is to be a name of an act. The argument is among the earliest sustained discussions in any language of the relationship between nouns and verbs.

## Etymology and Insight

A characteristic Nirukta etymology runs as follows. Yaska analyzes the divine name **Indra** (इन्द्र). He offers four possible derivations, drawn from different verbal roots:

- From the root *ind-* "to drop" — Indra drops the Soma into the heavens.
- From *idam dadāti* — "he gives this" (the rain).
- From *idam drāvayati* — "he makes this flow" (the waters).
- From *idam dṛṇāti* — "he splits this open" (the cloud).

Each derivation illuminates one aspect of Indra's mythological function. Yaska does not insist on choosing one; he treats the cluster of possible derivations as collectively expressing what Indra *means* in the Vedic imagination. The method is closer to medieval Christian *quadruple sense* exegesis than to modern philology — but it is sophisticated, principled, and clearly aware that words can carry multiple layers of meaning at once.

## The Three Categories of Words

Yaska classifies the words of the Vedic vocabulary into three groups:

- **Nāman** (noun) — designates substance.
- **Ākhyāta** (verb) — designates action.
- **Upasarga** and **Nipāta** — prepositions and particles, modifying the others.

This three-fold classification anticipates by centuries the Western grammatical tradition's parts-of-speech analysis and was foundational for Panini's own treatment.

## Legacy

The Nirukta has remained a working tool for Vedic exegesis for two and a half thousand years. Every traditional commentary on a Vedic hymn — from Sāyaṇa in the 14th century to contemporary scholars — draws on Yaska's etymologies. Modern Indology has often disagreed with Yaska's specific derivations (Western historical linguistics provides different and usually more rigorous etymologies), but Yaska's work as a record of how the Vedic tradition itself understood its own vocabulary remains indispensable. It is the closest thing we have to a Vedic dictionary written by a Vedic speaker.
