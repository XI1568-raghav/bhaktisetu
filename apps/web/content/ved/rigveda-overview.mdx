The **Rigveda** (ऋग्वेद, *Ṛgveda*) — literally the "Veda of Verses" — is the oldest of the four Vedas and the most ancient surviving composition in any Indo-European language. The Sanskrit *ṛc* means a stanza of praise meant for invocation, and the Rigveda is essentially a collection of such stanzas arranged into hymns (*sūkta*) addressed to the Vedic deities. The text in its received form contains 1,028 hymns comprising 10,552 verses, organized into ten books or **maṇḍalas** (मण्डल).

## Composition and Layering

The Rigveda is not a single-period composition. The so-called "family books" — Mandalas 2 through 7, each attributed to a specific Rishi lineage — form the oldest stratum and were likely composed between roughly 1500 and 1200 BCE. Mandala 9, devoted entirely to Soma Pavamana, is a thematic collection drawn from across the tradition. Mandalas 1 and 10, the longest and most varied, were finalized later and include the famous philosophical hymns of cosmic speculation. The text was then fixed through the *padapāṭha* of Shakalya around the sixth century BCE, and that recension is essentially the form we read today.

## Style and Language

The hymns are composed in archaic Vedic Sanskrit, a tonal language with three pitch accents whose precise pronunciation was preserved by oral schools. Their style is dense, allusive, and frequently riddling — what scholars call **brahmodya** (the cosmological riddle) is a recurring trope. The poets, called **kavis** (कवि, "seers" or "makers"), prized condensed metaphor and intricate metrical craft. Eleven principal meters are used, including the Gayatri (24 syllables) and Trishtubh (44 syllables).

## The Vedic Pantheon

The Rigveda addresses some 33 principal deities, traditionally grouped as eleven each in the heavens, atmosphere, and earth. The most-invoked are:

- **Indra** — warrior king of the gods, slayer of Vritra, receives roughly a quarter of all hymns.
- **Agni** — fire, the priest among gods, addressed in about 200 hymns including the very first.
- **Soma** — the ritual drink and its divinity, focus of the entire 9th Mandala.
- **Varuna** and **Mitra** — guardians of cosmic order (*ṛta*).
- **Ushas** — the dawn goddess, sung in some of the most lyrical hymns.
- **Ashvins** — twin horsemen, divine healers.

The world-view is dynamic rather than systematic. Different hymns elevate different gods, leading Max Müller to coin the term *henotheism* — one god at a time treated as supreme without denying others.

## Ritual Function

The Rigveda was the working scripture of the **hotṛ** (होतृ) priest, who recited invocations during sacrifice. While later Vedic religion elaborated complex rituals like the *agniṣṭoma* and *aśvamedha*, the Rigveda preserves an earlier and more direct mode of address: poet to god, with offerings of *havis* (oblation), *soma*, and praise.

## Place in the Tradition

For orthodox Hinduism the Rigveda is the foundational *śruti*. Its hymns supply the mantras still used in *saṃskāra* rites — including the *Gāyatrī Mantra* (3.62.10), perhaps the most widely chanted verse in the entire Sanskrit tradition. The Rigveda also contains the seeds of later philosophy: the *Nāsadīya Sūkta*'s questioning of creation, the *Puruṣa Sūkta*'s cosmic sacrifice, and the *Hiraṇyagarbha*'s golden womb of being. To read the Rigveda is to encounter the first articulate voice of Indian religious imagination.
