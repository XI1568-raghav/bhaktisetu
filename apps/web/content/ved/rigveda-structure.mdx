The Rigveda is one of the most elaborately organized texts in ancient literature. Its 1,028 hymns and 10,552 verses are arranged in nested units, and the tradition transmits two parallel counting systems — the thematic *maṇḍala* division used by reciters today and an older *aṣṭaka* division that survives mainly in manuscript pagination.

## The Maṇḍala Division

A *maṇḍala* (मण्डल, "circle" or "book") is the largest organizing unit. The Rigveda has ten:

- **Maṇḍala 1** — 191 hymns, attributed to 15 different *ṛṣis*; a late compilation.
- **Maṇḍalas 2–7** — the **family books**, each ascribed to a single Rishi clan: Gritsamada (2), Vishvamitra (3), Vamadeva (4), Atri (5), Bharadvaja (6), Vasishtha (7). These form the oldest stratum.
- **Maṇḍala 8** — 103 hymns of the Kanva and Angirasa families; transitional in age.
- **Maṇḍala 9** — 114 hymns devoted entirely to **Soma Pavamana**, drawn thematically from across the tradition rather than by family.
- **Maṇḍala 10** — 191 hymns, longest and latest; includes the philosophical *Nāsadīya*, *Puruṣa*, and *Hiraṇyagarbha* hymns, the *Yamī-Yama* dialogue, and the wedding and funeral hymns.

Within each Mandala the hymns are conventionally arranged by deity (Agni first, then Indra, then others) and within each deity-group by decreasing length, so that the structure itself encodes a hierarchy of liturgical importance.

## Sub-divisions

Each Maṇḍala is divided into **anuvākas** (अनुवाक, "lessons"), each anuvāka into individual **sūktas** (सूक्त, "well-spoken hymns"), and each sūkta into **ṛcs** or **mantras** — the individual verses. A typical citation reads, for example, Rigveda 3.62.10 — Mandala 3, Sukta 62, Verse 10 — the verse known as the Gayatri Mantra.

## The Aṣṭaka System

Older manuscripts use a different scheme: the entire Rigveda is divided into **eight aṣṭakas** (अष्टक), each of eight **adhyāyas** (chapters), each of *vargas* (groups of about five verses). The system is purely mechanical, designed for memorization in equal daily portions, and bears no relation to thematic content. Modern editions print both numbering systems in the margin.

## The Anukramaṇī Indices

A remarkable feature of Vedic preservation is the **anukramaṇī** (अनुक्रमणी), a metadata index. The *Sarvānukramaṇī* of Katyayana, compiled around the fourth century BCE, records for every single hymn its presiding deity, attributed Rishi, and meter. This index has allowed scholars to trace authorship, identify interpolations, and reconstruct the social geography of the composers — the Vasishthas associated with Varuna devotion, the Bharadvajas linked with the Yamuna basin, and so on.

## The Shakala Recension

By the late Vedic period the Rigveda existed in several **shakhas** (शाखा, "branches" or recensions). Tradition names twenty-one branches, of which only the **Śākala-śākhā** (शाकलशाखा) survives in full living transmission, and a fragment of the **Bāṣkala-śākhā** is also preserved. The Shakala text is what every modern edition — from the 1849 editio princeps of Max Müller onward — prints. The lost branches likely differed in minor ordering and in the inclusion of a small number of *khila* (supplementary) hymns.

## Why the Structure Matters

The architecture is not ornament. It mirrors a memorization plan, a priestly social map, and a developmental history of the text. To read across the Mandalas is to traverse roughly three centuries of poetic and ritual evolution, and the structure itself is the first commentary the tradition offers on its own scripture.
