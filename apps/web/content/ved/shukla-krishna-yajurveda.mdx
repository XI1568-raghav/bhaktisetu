Unlike the Rigveda, which is preserved in essentially a single living recension, the Yajurveda exists in two strikingly different forms: the **Śukla** ("White") Yajurveda and the **Kṛṣṇa** ("Black") Yajurveda. The names sound dramatic, but in scholarly terms they encode a difference of editorial principle rather than of theological content.

## The Editorial Distinction

The fundamental contrast lies in how each recension organizes mantra and explanation. In the **Krishna Yajurveda** the *yajus* mantras and the explanatory **Brāhmaṇa** passages are interwoven — the priest meets a mantra, then a brief gloss on its use, then the next mantra, in a continuous flow. In the **Shukla Yajurveda** the mantras are gathered into a separate Samhita (the *Vājasaneyi Saṃhitā*) and the explanations are removed to a separate Brahmana text (the immense *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa*). The Krishna form is older; the Shukla represents a later editorial intervention that segregated the two genres.

The names reflect the editorial result. The Krishna ("Black") recension was felt to be "dark" or "mixed" because the mantras and their elaborations are intertwined; the Shukla ("White") recension is "clear" or "well-arranged" because the mantras stand by themselves. Some traditional commentators inverted this aesthetic into a theological judgment — claiming the Shukla is the better-organized and therefore superior form — but no textual hierarchy is recognized by all schools.

## The Yājñavalkya Legend

The Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa and several Puranas preserve a colorful etiological story. **Vaiśampāyana**, the original teacher of the Yajurveda, was offended by his disciple **Yājñavalkya**. As penance Vaiśampāyana demanded that Yājñavalkya "vomit forth" everything he had learned. The other disciples, taking the form of *tittiri* (partridge) birds, picked up the regurgitated text — and this is the **Taittirīya Saṃhitā**, the principal Krishna recension. Yājñavalkya then performed *tapas* to the Sun god Sūrya, who taught him a new and clearer version of the Yajurveda — the **Vājasaneyi Saṃhitā**, called Shukla because received from the bright Sun.

The legend should not be taken historically, but it preserves three real facts: that the Shukla recension is later, that it is associated with the lineage of Yājñavalkya, and that the tradition itself recognized a fork in transmission.

## Surviving Branches

The two recensions have very different geographical footprints.

**Shukla Yajurveda** survives in two living *śākhās*:
- **Mādhyandina** — dominant in north India (Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Maharashtra). Forty Adhyayas.
- **Kāṇva** — predominant in south India (Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Andhra) and in pockets of Maharashtra. Forty Adhyayas with minor variations.

**Krishna Yajurveda** survives in four recensions, of which one is dominant:
- **Taittirīya** — overwhelmingly the most widely studied, especially in Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Karnataka, and Andhra.
- **Maitrāyaṇī** — surviving in parts of Gujarat and Maharashtra.
- **Kāṭhaka** — once dominant in Kashmir, now largely a manuscript tradition.
- **Kapiṣṭhala** — preserved only in fragments.

## Affiliated Texts

The two recensions carry different supplementary literatures.

The **Śukla** lineage produced the *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* — "the Brahmana of a Hundred Paths," among the longest prose works in Sanskrit. From the Shatapatha derive the **Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad** (the longest Upanishad and the home of Yājñavalkya's great dialogues) and the **Īśā Upaniṣad** (which appears as chapter 40 of the Vājasaneyi Samhita itself — uniquely among Upanishads, it is part of the Samhita).

The **Kṛṣṇa** lineage produced the *Taittirīya Brāhmaṇa*, *Taittirīya Āraṇyaka*, and *Taittirīya Upaniṣad*, the *Maitrāyaṇī Upaniṣad*, and the influential *Kaṭha Upaniṣad* (affiliated with the Katha branch).

## Practical Continuity

A south Indian Smarta Brahmin will typically recite the **Krishna Taittirīya** for daily ritual; a north Indian Smarta Brahmin will typically recite the **Shukla Madhyandina**. Both communities consider the other's recension equally authoritative, and a learned Vedavid is expected to know the broad organization of both. The two-fold form of the Yajurveda is thus less a schism than a feature — the same liturgical tradition preserved in two complementary archives.
