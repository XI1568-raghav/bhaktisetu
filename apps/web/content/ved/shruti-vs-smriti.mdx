Hindu textual tradition is organized around a fundamental classification: **śruti** (शृति), "that which is heard," and **smṛti** (स्मृति), "that which is remembered." The distinction is not merely chronological — it encodes a hierarchy of religious authority that has shaped commentarial culture for more than two millennia.

## Shruti — The Revealed Texts

Shruti refers to literature regarded as eternal and impersonal — *apauruṣeya* (अपौरुषेय), "not of human origin." In the orthodox view the *ṛṣis* did not compose the Vedic mantras; they *heard* them when, through ascetic discipline, their inner perception aligned with the timeless sound-current of the cosmos. Shruti therefore enjoys the highest scriptural authority. The shruti corpus consists exclusively of:

- The four **Samhitas** — Rig, Yajur, Sama, Atharva.
- The **Brahmanas** attached to each Samhita.
- The **Aranyakas**, the forest-treatise layer.
- The **Upanishads**, the philosophical dialogues.

Together these four layers constitute the Vedic canon proper. The Mimamsa school went so far as to argue that the Vedas have no author at all — neither human nor divine — and that the sound (*śabda*) and meaning of Vedic statements are co-eternal.

## Smriti — The Remembered Tradition

Smriti by contrast is acknowledged human composition. It is held to be authoritative only insofar as it agrees with shruti, but it is enormously richer in narrative, ethical guidance, devotion, and daily practice. The smriti corpus includes:

- The **Itihasa** — the *Mahabharata* and *Ramayana*.
- The **Puranas** — eighteen major (*mahāpurāṇa*) and eighteen minor (*upapurāṇa*) compendia.
- The **Dharmashastra** — legal and ethical codes such as *Manusmriti*, *Yajnavalkya Smriti*, and *Naradasmriti*.
- The **Vedanga** — six auxiliary disciplines (phonetics, meter, grammar, etymology, astronomy, ritual).
- The **Agamas** and **Tantras** — sectarian scriptures of Shaiva, Vaishnava, and Shakta traditions.
- The **darśana sūtras** — the foundational aphorisms of the six orthodox philosophical schools.

For most lived Hindu religion smriti is the daily reference. Few worshippers can recite the Rigveda, but millions know the *Bhagavad Gita* (a section of the *Mahabharata*) or the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* intimately.

## The Hierarchy in Practice

When shruti and smriti appear to conflict, the principle stated by Manu and reaffirmed by Shankara is that shruti prevails. The famous formulation runs: श्रुतिस्मृतिविरोधे तु श्रुतिरेव गरीयसी (*śrutismṛtivirodhe tu śrutireva garīyasī*) — "in a conflict between shruti and smriti, shruti is weightier." Commentators, however, often work hard to harmonize the two, treating apparent contradictions as differences of context rather than substance.

## Boundary Cases

Some texts straddle the categories. The *Bhagavad Gita*, formally smriti, is treated as functional shruti by the Vedanta tradition — Shankara, Ramanuja, and Madhva all wrote commentaries on it as one of the three foundational *prasthāna-trayī* texts alongside the Upanishads and the *Brahma Sutras*. The *Devi Mahatmya* enjoys similar elevated status in Shakta practice. Such cases show that the shruti–smriti distinction, while doctrinally sharp, is in lived tradition permeable: authority flows where devotion and commentary direct it.
