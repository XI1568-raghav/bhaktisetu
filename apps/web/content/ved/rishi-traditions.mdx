The Rigveda was not composed by a single author. Tradition names hundreds of individual *ṛṣis* (ऋषि) — seers — who "saw" the hymns in inspired vision and transmitted them within their families. The *Sarvānukramaṇī* of Katyayana, the late Vedic metadata index, records an attribution for every one of the 1,028 hymns. From these attributions a remarkable picture of clan-based poetic transmission emerges.

## The Family Books

Mandalas 2 through 7 of the Rigveda are called the **family books** (*vamsha-mandalas*) because each was preserved by a single Rishi clan. The traditional ascription is:

- **Maṇḍala 2** — the Gṛtsamadas
- **Maṇḍala 3** — the Vishvāmitras (Kuśika clan)
- **Maṇḍala 4** — the Vāmadevas (Gautama clan)
- **Maṇḍala 5** — the Atris
- **Maṇḍala 6** — the Bharadvājas
- **Maṇḍala 7** — the Vasiṣṭhas

Each book preserves a recognizable "house style" of imagery, favored deities, and metrical preference. The Vasishtha hymns of Mandala 7 are notably devoted to Varuna and to Mitra-Varuna together; the Bharadvaja book is closely concerned with Agni; the Vishvamitras of Mandala 3 are the lineage that preserves the famous *Gāyatrī Mantra* (RV 3.62.10).

## Geographical Anchoring

Michael Witzel's reconstruction of the Rigvedic geography, drawing on river references in each Mandala, places the family clans in different parts of the northwestern subcontinent in the second millennium BCE. The Bharadvajas appear to have lived along the upper Sarasvati, the Vishvamitras around the Beas–Sutlej basin, the Vasishthas near the Ravi. The hymns therefore preserve not only theological poetry but a fragmentary atlas of the early Vedic settlement of the Punjab.

## The Vishvamitra–Vasishtha Rivalry

One of the most vivid threads in the Rigveda is the rivalry between Vishvāmitra and Vasiṣṭha, both of whom served as *purohitas* (royal chaplains) to the Bharata king Sudās. According to the hymns of Mandala 7, Sudās dismissed Vishvamitra in favor of Vasishtha, and Vishvamitra subsequently allied with a coalition of ten kings against Sudās in the famous **Battle of the Ten Kings** (*dāśarājña*, RV 7.18). Sudās won; the Vasishthas commemorated the victory; later Puranic literature elaborated the rivalry into a vast cycle of stories.

## The Saptarṣi Tradition

Later tradition crystallized seven of the Rigvedic Rishis into the **Saptarṣi** (सप्तर्षि, "Seven Sages"), identified with the seven stars of Ursa Major. Several lists exist; the most common gives **Atri, Bharadvāja, Gautama, Jamadagni, Kaśyapa, Vasiṣṭha, and Viśvāmitra**. The Shatapatha Brahmana offers a different list with Vishvamitra replaced by others. Subsequent Hindu cosmology grouped the Saptarshi as immortal beings appointed for each *manvantara* — a long cosmic age — giving the seven Vedic poets a cosmological as well as historical role.

## Women Rishis

The Rigveda preserves the names of about thirty women composers of hymns, the *ṛṣikās* — including **Ghoṣā** (RV 10.39), **Lopāmudrā** (consort of Agastya, RV 1.179), **Romaśā**, **Apālā** (RV 8.91), **Sūryā Sāvitrī** (the wedding hymn RV 10.85), and **Vāc Ambhṛṇī**, who composed the powerful *Devī Sūkta* (RV 10.125) — a first-person hymn in which the goddess of speech proclaims her own sovereignty. These names show that the early Rigvedic milieu, whatever its later social codification, was not exclusively male in its poetic culture.

## The Rishi Function

What did it mean to be a Rishi? The hymns themselves describe the seer as one whose "vision" (*manīṣā*) is sharpened by *tapas* and by exact knowledge of the metrical and ritual craft. The hymn is not made but *seen* — and once seen, transmitted with precision to the next generation of the family. The continuity of these lineages, from second-millennium-BCE poets through late Vedic recensors to the modern Veda pathshala student, is one of the longest unbroken cultural threads in human history.
