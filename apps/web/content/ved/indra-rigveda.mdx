**Indra** (इन्द्र) is the most-invoked deity of the Rigveda. Roughly 250 of the 1,028 hymns — nearly a quarter — are addressed to him alone, and he appears prominently in scores of others. To the Vedic poets he is *vajrabhṛt* (the wielder of the thunderbolt), *purandara* (breaker of fortresses), *vṛtrahan* (slayer of Vritra), and *śacīpati* (lord of might). He embodies the martial energy and ritual potency on which the Vedic worshipper most often called.

## The Central Myth — Vritra

The defining act of Indra is the slaying of **Vṛtra** (वृत्र), the cosmic serpent who has dammed up the waters and withheld the rains. The myth is recounted in numerous hymns, most famously Rigveda 1.32, attributed to the Rishi Hiranyastupa:

इन्द्रस्य नु वीर्याणि प्र वोचं यानि चकार प्रथमानि वज्री ।
*indrasya nu vīryāṇi pra vocaṃ yāni cakāra prathamāni vajrī*
"I shall now proclaim the heroic deeds of Indra, the foremost that the bearer of the thunderbolt performed."

Strengthened by Soma, Indra hurls his *vajra* (वज्र, thunderbolt) — forged for him by Tvaṣṭṛ — and shatters Vritra. The waters, called "the seven rivers," flow free, the sun mounts the sky, and the cosmos resumes its proper rhythm. The myth is at once meteorological (the breaking of monsoon clouds), cosmogonic (the establishment of cosmic order), and socio-political (the warrior-king securing the resources of the community).

## Attributes and Iconography

Indra is described as tawny-bearded (*hari-śmaśru*), with a body the color of gold, riding a chariot drawn by two bay horses named **Hari** and **Hari**. His weapon is the *vajra*, sometimes glossed as the thunderbolt and sometimes as a club of metal. He drinks enormous quantities of Soma — entire lakes of it, in the hymn's hyperbolic vein — and grows strong enough to fill the space between earth and heaven.

His consort is **Indrāṇī** or **Śacī**. He commands the Maruts, the storm-troop of howling warriors who accompany him into battle. His sworn enemies are the *dāsas* and *dasyus*, often interpreted as both demonic figures and human adversaries of the Vedic clans.

## Beyond the Vritra Myth

Indra performs many other deeds in the Rigveda. He props apart heaven and earth, releases the cows from the cave where the Panis have hidden them, defeats Shambara and Pipru, and assists the Aryan kings in war. The famous **Battle of the Ten Kings** (*dāśarājña*, Rigveda 7.18) describes how Indra aided the Bharata king Sudas against a coalition of ten enemy clans on the river Paruṣṇī (modern Ravi).

## The Soma Connection

Indra and Soma are inseparable. Many hymns describe Indra's drinking of the pressed Soma as the very source of his world-ordering power. The *somayāga* sacrifice — the ritual squeezing, filtering, and offering of the Soma juice — is in large part an offering to Indra, whose hymns of praise sustain the cosmic order he has won.

## The Decline

In the later Vedic and Puranic periods Indra's status diminishes. He becomes, in the Brahmanas, a god whose victories are no longer self-evidently righteous (he is repeatedly described as committing brahminicide by killing Vritra, who was nominally a Brahmin). In the Puranas he is humbled by Vishnu and Shiva, defeated by demons like Ravana's son Meghanada (who earns the name Indrajit, "conqueror of Indra"), and reduced to a regent of the eastern direction. The shift mirrors a broader theological transformation: the warrior-king of the early Aryans gives way to the cosmic sovereignty of the great Puranic deities.

Yet in the Rigveda itself Indra stands undiminished — the bright, drunken, exuberant champion of cosmic order, sung in some of the most vigorous poetry the Sanskrit tradition would ever produce.
