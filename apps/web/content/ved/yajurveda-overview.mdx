The **Yajurveda** (यजुर्वेद) is the second of the four Vedas — the Veda of the ritual formula. Its name combines *yajus*, a prose mantra used to direct a sacrificial action, with *veda*, knowledge. While the Rigveda supplies the verses of praise sung by the *hotṛ* priest and the Samaveda supplies the melodies of the *udgātṛ*, the Yajurveda is the working manual of the **adhvaryu** (अध्वर्यु), the priest who physically performs the actions of the *yajña* — measuring the altar, kindling the fire, pouring the oblation, leading the sacrificial animal, pressing the Soma.

## A Different Kind of Veda

The Yajurveda differs from the Rigveda in two important respects. First, much of it is in **prose** rather than metrical verse — the *yajus* formula is typically a short prose sentence to be murmured while a ritual act is performed. Second, the text **interleaves mantra with explanation**: alongside the formula one finds passages of Brahmana commentary explaining when, why, and how each act is to be done. In the case of the Krishna (Black) recension, mantra and commentary are mixed together; in the Shukla (White) recension they are separated.

## Two Great Recensions

The Yajurveda is preserved in two main branches:

- The **Śukla** (White) Yajurveda, transmitted through the **Vājasaneyi Saṃhitā** in two sub-recensions — Madhyandina (predominant in north India) and Kāṇva (predominant in Maharashtra and the south). It has 40 chapters of pure mantra; its Brahmana is the great **Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa**.
- The **Kṛṣṇa** (Black) Yajurveda, preserved in four recensions: **Taittirīya**, **Maitrāyaṇī**, **Kāṭhaka**, and **Kapiṣṭhala**. Of these the Taittirīya is by far the most widely used today, especially in south India.

Tradition explains the names through the legend of Yājñavalkya and his teacher Vaiśampāyana, but the linguistic and structural reality is that the Krishna recensions preserve an older organizational state — mantra and Brahmana intertwined — while the Shukla represents a later editorial clarification that separated them.

## Contents

The Yajurveda Samhita supplies the mantras for the great public *śrauta* sacrifices that defined late Vedic religion: the **darśapūrṇamāsa** (new and full moon offerings), the **agnihotra** (twice-daily fire offering), the **agniṣṭoma** and other *somayāgas*, the **aśvamedha** (horse sacrifice), the **rājasūya** (royal consecration), and the **vājapeya** (drink of strength). Its language is technical and dense — long lists of deities to be invoked, ingredients to be offered, directions to be faced, and gestures to be made.

## Famous Sections

Even within so technical a text, certain passages have become independent objects of devotion:

- The **Rudrādhyāya** or **Śrī Rudram** (Krishna Yajurveda, Taittirīya Saṃhitā 4.5 and 4.7) — the great hymn to Rudra-Shiva, central to Shaiva liturgy.
- The **Puruṣa Sūkta** — included in the Vājasaneyi Saṃhitā 31.
- The **Camakam** — a hymn of enumerated blessings recited after the Rudram.
- The **Maitrī** verses on universal benevolence.

## Attached Texts

The Yajurveda has the richest set of attached Brahmana, Aranyaka, and Upanishad layers of any Veda. The **Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa** of the Shukla branch is perhaps the longest single prose text in Sanskrit. The **Taittirīya Brāhmaṇa**, **Taittirīya Āraṇyaka**, and **Taittirīya Upaniṣad** of the Krishna branch form a continuous reading sequence in many Vedic pathshalas. The **Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad** (the longest Upanishad), the **Īśa Upaniṣad**, and the **Kaṭha Upaniṣad** are all affiliated with branches of the Yajurveda.

## Living Tradition

Yajurvedic recitation is the most widely practiced Vedic tradition today. The majority of Smarta Brahmins of south India belong to the Taittirīya Krishna Yajurveda lineage; the Vājasaneyi Mādhyandina Shukla Yajurveda predominates among northern Brahmin communities. Yajurvedic mantras structure daily *sandhyāvandanam*, *nitya karma*, household *havan*, and major life-cycle rites. To be present at a traditional Hindu ritual today is, more often than not, to be hearing the Yajurveda murmured.
