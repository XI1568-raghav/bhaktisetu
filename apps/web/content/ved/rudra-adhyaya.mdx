The **Rudrādhyāya** (रुद्राध्याय) — popularly known as the **Śrī Rudram** or simply *Rudram* — is among the most important hymns of the entire Vedic corpus and the central liturgical text of Shaiva worship. It appears in the **Taittirīya Saṃhitā** of the Krishna Yajurveda as chapters 4.5 (the **Namakam**) and 4.7 (the **Chamakam**), and it is also preserved with minor variations in the *Maitrāyaṇī* and *Kāṭhaka* Samhitas as well as in the Rigveda (parts of the Rudra hymns of Mandala 1 and 7 are textually related).

## The Two Halves

The Rudra liturgy is conventionally divided into two parts, each containing eleven *anuvākas* (lessons).

- The **Śrī Rudram Namakam** (श्री रुद्रं नमकम्) — every mantra contains the salutation *namo* (नमः), "obeisance to."
- The **Śrī Rudram Chamakam** (श्री रुद्रं चमकम्) — every mantra contains the word *ca me* (च मे), "and to me," requesting specific blessings.

The contrast is theologically rich. The Namakam is purely worshipful — *namo namo* repeated in every conceivable formulation. The Chamakam is openly petitionary — a comprehensive list of every good thing the worshipper desires, asked of the very deity to whom he has just bowed.

## Opening of the Namakam

The first verse, perhaps the single most-recited Vedic mantra in Shaiva liturgy:

ॐ नमो भगवते रुद्राय ।
*oṃ namo bhagavate rudrāya*
"Om, salutation to Lord Rudra."

The most famous mantra of all comes a few verses later — the **Pañcākṣarī**, the five-syllable mantra concealed within the Namakam:

नमः शिवाय च शिवतराय च ।
*namaḥ śivāya ca śivatarāya ca*
"Salutation to the Auspicious One, and to the Most Auspicious."

The five syllables *namaḥ śivāya* (नमः शिवाय) extracted from this verse became, in later Shaiva tradition, the supreme mantra of devotion — the *Pañcākṣarī Mantra* whispered into the disciple's ear at initiation.

## Theological Vision

The Namakam praises Rudra in every aspect of existence. He is the lord of forests and of cities, of weapons and of healing, of warriors and of carpenters, of thieves and of householders. The hymn famously addresses him as both the source of fearful disease and the source of all medicine — Rudra is the god whose terrible aspect (*ghora*) and benign aspect (*aghora*) are inseparable, two faces of a single sovereign reality. Long lists of professions, terrains, tools, and conditions are saluted, each closed with *namo namaḥ*: the cosmos itself is Rudra, and there is no place where his salutation does not apply.

## The Chamakam

The Chamakam asks for an extraordinary range of blessings. The petitioner requests food, drink, strength, prosperity, long life, sons, cattle, gold, knowledge, the company of the good, freedom from sin and from death. Each item is yoked to *ca me yajñena kalpantām* — "and may these be granted to me through this sacrifice." Western readers sometimes find the catalog astonishingly worldly; the Indian tradition reads it as the candid honesty of Vedic religion, which does not separate material flourishing from spiritual aspiration.

## The Mahāmṛtyuñjaya Connection

The famous **Mahāmṛtyuñjaya Mantra** — *tryambakaṃ yajāmahe* — is technically a Rigvedic verse (RV 7.59.12), but it is conventionally chanted within the Rudra liturgy, especially before the Namakam, as an invocation of Rudra in his three-eyed form.

## Recitation in Practice

The Shri Rudram is recited in Shiva temples across India daily, with special elaboration on Mondays, during the month of Shravan, and on Mahashivaratri. The most powerful liturgical form is the **Rudra Ekadaśinī** — the Namakam recited eleven times, alternating with the Chamakam — and its multiplied forms: **Lagu Rudram** (eleven Ekadaśinīs), **Mahā Rudram** (eleven Lagus), and **Atirudram** (eleven Mahā Rudrams), each requiring large gatherings of priests over many days. To attend even one full recitation of the Shri Rudram is to encounter the Vedic voice in continuous waves of namo-namaḥ — a hymn at once cosmically expansive and intimately devotional.
