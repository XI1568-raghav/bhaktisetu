Indian medical tradition reckons four classical Vedas, but only one of them has historically been considered the root of medicine: the **Atharvaveda** (अथर्ववेद). The major Ayurvedic compendia — the *Caraka Saṃhitā* and the *Suśruta Saṃhitā* — explicitly identify the Atharvaveda as the source from which the science of medicine descends. The Charaka Samhita opens its discussion of the topic by stating that **Āyurveda** is an **upaveda** (a supplementary Veda) of the Atharva.

## The Bhaiṣajya Category

The Atharvaveda contains a large class of hymns called **bhaiṣajya** (भैषज्य, "healing"). These mantras address specific diseases, often invoking divine assistance and naming herbs by name. Among the conditions explicitly addressed are:

- **Takmán** (तक्मन्) — fever, the single most-treated affliction in the Atharvaveda, with detailed sub-categories (intermittent, periodic, etc.).
- **Yakṣma** (यक्ष्म) — wasting disease, possibly tuberculosis or a broader cluster of consumptive illnesses.
- **Hariman** — jaundice, treated in the famous hymn AV 1.22 which sends the yellow color into yellow birds and herbs.
- **Kuṣṭha** — skin disease (used both for the condition and for the herb of the same name said to cure it).
- **Krimi** — worms, with mantras to expel them from the body.
- **Snake bite, scorpion sting, arrow wounds, head injury** — each with its own hymn.
- **Insanity and "possession"** — addressed by hymns binding the troubling agency.
- **Difficult labor and post-partum complications.**

## A Famous Healing Verse

The jaundice hymn (AV 1.22) is among the most quoted, with its striking image of color-transference:

अनु सूर्यमुदयतां हृद्द्योतो हरिमा च ते ।
गो रोहितस्य वर्णेन तेन त्वा परि दध्मसि ॥
*anu sūryam ud ayatāṃ hṛd-dyoto harimā ca te*
*go-rohitasya varṇena tena tvā pari dadhmasi*

"Let your heart's distress and your jaundice rise away with the sun; with the color of the red bull we surround you."

The patient is meant to be wrapped in the color and protection of the red, while the yellow disease is dispatched into yellow parrots and into the yellow turmeric plant — a vivid early instance of sympathetic medicine.

## Medicinal Herbs

The Atharvaveda names roughly 290 plants used for medicinal purposes — the earliest extensive pharmacopoeia in any Indian text. Among them are *kuṣṭha*, *apāmārga* (Achyranthes aspera), *arundhatī* (a name given to several creepers used as wound-healers), *darbha* (Desmostachya bipinnata), *guggulu*, *somavalka*, and *kṣetriya* — and many that have not yet been securely identified with modern botanical species. Several hymns are dedicated to specific plants and describe their efficacy at length; the herb is addressed almost as a deity, asked to bring its healing power to the patient.

## Indication of Surgical Awareness

While the Atharvaveda is fundamentally a text of mantra-medicine rather than surgery, several hymns suggest awareness of practices that the later surgical school of **Suśruta** would systematize. AV 4.12 addresses the healing of a broken bone — "let marrow come together with marrow, joint with joint" — in language that anticipates the orthopedic concerns of the Sushruta Samhita. Other hymns address the dressing of wounds and the staunching of blood.

## Continuity with Classical Ayurveda

When the *Caraka Saṃhitā* was compiled around the early centuries CE, its authors looked back to the Atharvaveda as their warrant. Charaka Sutrasthana 30 explicitly lists the Atharvaveda among the four Vedas a physician must respect, and treats Ayurveda as descending from it. The compendia of Charaka and Sushruta vastly extend the Atharvavedic material — adding anatomy, surgery, pharmacology, and elaborate theory of the *doṣa* (humors) — but they preserve and rationalize the older inheritance of plant knowledge.

The relationship between mantra-healing and clinical medicine remained close throughout the classical period: even the most rationally framed Ayurvedic treatment was often accompanied by recitation of the relevant Atharvavedic mantra, and Charaka explicitly endorses *daiva-vyapāśraya* (divine-recourse) therapies alongside *yukti-vyapāśraya* (rational therapies). The Atharvaveda thus stands not as a primitive precursor to Ayurveda but as its acknowledged scriptural root — a continuous tradition of healing that traces itself back, in unbroken transmission, more than three thousand years.
