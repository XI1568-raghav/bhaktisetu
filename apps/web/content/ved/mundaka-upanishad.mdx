The **Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad** (मुण्डकोपनिषद्) — affiliated with the Atharvaveda — is among the most influential of the principal Upanishads, having supplied modern India with its national motto and Vedanta with several of its most enduring images. Its name means "shaved" and is traditionally explained as referring to the *muṇḍaka* class of ascetics (the shaven-headed renouncers) for whom it was originally intended. In three chapters (*muṇḍakas*), each of two sections (*khaṇḍas*), the text presents in compact verse a complete vision of Vedantic teaching.

## The Lineage

The Upanishad opens by tracing its teaching lineage to **Brahmā** himself, who gave it to his eldest son **Atharvan**, who passed it to Angir, who passed it to Bharadvāja, who passed it to Aṅgiras, from whom **Śaunaka** the great householder received it. Shaunaka approached Angiras with the celebrated question: *kasmin nu bhagavo vijñāte sarvam idaṃ vijñātaṃ bhavati* — "What is that, sir, knowing which all this becomes known?"

The question is the Upanishad's hinge. It assumes that there is a single knowable whose knowledge subsumes all other knowing, and it asks for it.

## Higher and Lower Knowledge

The reply makes a crucial distinction:

द्वे विद्ये वेदितव्ये इति ह स्म यद् ब्रह्मविदो वदन्ति परा चैवापरा च ।
*dve vidye veditavye iti ha sma yad brahmavido vadanti parā caivāparā ca*
"Two knowledges are to be known — so say those who know brahman — the higher (*parā*) and the lower (*aparā*)."

The lower knowledge, the Upanishad explains, includes the four Vedas (Rig, Yajur, Sama, Atharva), the six Vedangas (phonetics, ritual, grammar, etymology, prosody, astronomy) — that is, the entire educational curriculum of the orthodox tradition. The higher knowledge is that by which the imperishable (*akṣara*) is attained.

The classification is not dismissive of the lower. It positions the entire Vedic learning system as preparation, while pointing beyond it. This single distinction will become foundational to Vedanta's entire approach to scripture: the texts are necessary but penultimate.

## The Two Birds

The most beloved image of the Mundaka — and one that appears, with slight variation, also in the Rigveda 1.164 and the Shvetashvatara — comes in 3.1.1:

द्वा सुपर्णा सयुजा सखाया समानं वृक्षं परिषस्वजाते ।
तयोरन्यः पिप्पलं स्वाद्वत्त्यनश्नन्नन्यो अभिचाकशीति ॥
*dvā suparṇā sayujā sakhāyā samānaṃ vṛkṣaṃ pariṣasvajāte*
*tayor anyaḥ pippalaṃ svādv atty anaśnann anyo abhicākaśīti*

"Two birds, companions joined together, perch on the same tree. One eats the sweet berry; the other looks on without eating."

The two birds are the individual self (*jīva*) and the supreme self (*paramātman*), inhabiting the same body (the tree). One is enmeshed in experience, eating its sweet and bitter fruits; the other is the witness, beholding without participation. The verse that follows promises liberation: when the eater bird perceives the watching bird and recognizes its identity with the watcher, his sorrow ceases.

The image has been commented on by every major Vedantin and has been adapted in poetry, art, and modern religious imagination for two and a half millennia.

## Satyam Eva Jayate

From Mundaka 3.1.6 comes the phrase inscribed on the State Emblem of India and used as the national motto:

सत्यमेव जयते नानृतं सत्येन पन्था विततो देवयानः ।
*satyam eva jayate nānṛtaṃ satyena panthā vitato devayānaḥ*
"Truth alone triumphs, not falsehood. By truth is laid out the path of the gods."

The verse continues that it is by truth (*satya*), by *tapas*, by perfect knowledge, and by *brahmacarya* that the seers attain the supreme dwelling. The motto inscribed at the base of the Ashoka Lion Capital takes only the first three words, but the entire verse situates truth not as one virtue among many but as the road on which the gods themselves travel.

## The Critique of Ritualism

Mundaka 1.2 contains one of the sharpest critiques of *karma-kāṇḍa* (the ritual portion of the Veda) anywhere in scripture:

प्लवा ह्येते अदृढा यज्ञरूपाः ।
*plavā hy ete adṛḍhā yajñarūpāḥ*
"These rafts in the form of sacrifices are insecure."

The fools who delight in the rituals and call them the highest good, says the Upanishad, are like the blind led by the blind. They reach a heaven that perishes; they return again to the world. Only the seekers of brahman by tranquility, austerity, and faith reach the imperishable. The passage is striking for its appearance within a Vedic text — it is the Veda itself relativizing the Veda.

## Reception

The Mundaka has been a favorite of every Vedantic ācārya. Shankara's commentary is brief and densely argued; Ramanuja and Madhva both draw on its verses to support their distinct readings of brahman. In the modern period the Mundaka has provided the Indian state with its motto, Mahatma Gandhi with reference points for *satyāgraha*, and Vivekananda with material for some of his most rousing addresses. Few Upanishads of such modest length have left so wide a mark.
