The **Praśna Upaniṣad** (प्रश्नोपनिषद्) — literally "the Upanishad of Questions" — belongs to the Atharvaveda, specifically the *Paippalāda* tradition. The text takes a strikingly orderly form: six disciples approach the sage **Pippalāda**, each asks one question, and the teaching of the Upanishad consists of his six replies. The six questions move from cosmogony outward to detailed psychology and inward to the supreme syllable OM and the *puruṣa* who underlies all.

## The Setting

The Upanishad opens with six seekers — Sukeśā, Saibya Satyakāma, Sauryāyaṇī Gārgya, Kausalya Aśvalāyana, Bhārgava Vaidarbhi, and Kabandhi Kātyāyana — approaching Pippalāda intent on knowing brahman. The sage tells them to spend one year with him in *tapas*, *brahmacarya*, and *śraddhā* before he will answer. After the year, they ask their questions and receive his teaching. The narrative pattern emphasizes that even formulated questions require preparation; one must earn the ability to hear an answer.

## The Six Questions

The questions and the substance of Pippalada's replies are:

### 1. Whence are these creatures born? (Kabandhi)

Pippalada replies with a cosmogonic account: **Prajāpati** desired offspring; he created the **pair** — *rayi* (matter) and *prāṇa* (life-breath) — from which all manifest beings issue. Rayi is the food, prana is the eater; from their pairing all forms arise. The teaching incidentally identifies the lunar year with the path of the ancestors (*pitṛyāna*) and the solar year with the path of the gods (*devayāna*) — two destinies post-mortem that the *Chāndogya* and *Bṛhadāraṇyaka* also describe.

### 2. How many powers sustain the body? (Bhargava)

The reply names the elemental gods that uphold the body: ether, air, fire, water, earth, and the senses with the mind. They quarreled over precedence — until **prāṇa** withdrew briefly, and the body began to fail. They acknowledged prana's primacy. The teaching makes prana the central life-principle without which the senses and elements cannot function.

### 3. Whence comes prana itself? (Kausalya)

Pippalada describes the structure of the breath. Prana arises from the *ātman* as a shadow from a person. It then differentiates into five *prāṇas*: **prāṇa** (in-breath), **apāna** (out-breath, downward), **vyāna** (diffused through the body), **samāna** (assimilation), and **udāna** (upward, especially at death). The body is described as having 101 *nāḍīs* (channels), each ramifying into a hundred more — anticipating the later yogic anatomy.

### 4. Who in this person sleeps and wakes? (Sauryayani Gargya)

The reply distinguishes three states. In waking, the senses are active in the world. In dreaming, they have withdrawn into the mind but the mind remains active, creating its own world. In deep dreamless sleep (*suṣupti*), even the mind has gathered into the *ātman*; all is one, and the sleeper enjoys pure bliss without object. This three-fold analysis prepares the way for the Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad's fourth state, *turīya*.

### 5. What is gained by meditation on the syllable OM? (Saibya Satyakama)

OM, says Pippalada, is both the *para* (higher) and the *apara* (lower) brahman. Meditation on its first *mātrā* (the sound A alone) gains the human world; on its first two (AU) gains the intermediate; on all three (AUM) leads to the highest world, brahman itself. The meditator who knows OM as one — gathered into a single sound — sees the *puruṣa* in the heart and becomes free. The teaching closely anticipates the Māṇḍūkya.

### 6. Where is the *puruṣa* with sixteen parts? (Sukesha)

This last and most extended question receives the most elaborate reply. The *puruṣa* with sixteen parts is the cosmic Person who manifests as the elements, the senses, the powers of life, and finally the inner self. Pippalada describes how the sixteen parts emerge from the supreme puruṣa as rivers emerge from a great river — and how, in the knower's liberation, they return into him as those rivers return to the sea, losing their separate names. The famous closing simile:

यथा नद्यः स्यन्दमानाः समुद्रेऽस्तं गच्छन्ति नामरूपे विहाय ।
तथा विद्वान्नामरूपाद्विमुक्तः परात्परं पुरुषमुपैति दिव्यम् ॥
*yathā nadyaḥ syandamānāḥ samudre'staṃ gacchanti nāmarūpe vihāya*
*tathā vidvān nāmarūpād vimuktaḥ parātparaṃ puruṣam upaiti divyam*

"As flowing rivers, on reaching the ocean, vanish — abandoning name and form — so the wise, freed from name and form, attain the divine Person beyond the beyond."

## Reception and Significance

The Prashna's question-and-answer form, its compact treatment of cosmogony, the doctrine of the breaths, the three states of consciousness, and the meditation on OM, make it function almost as a syllabus of Upanishadic concerns. Shankara wrote a major *bhāṣya* on it; later Vedanta commentators have continued to draw on its concise formulations. It belongs, with the *Muṇḍaka* and *Māṇḍūkya*, to the principal Upanishads of the Atharvaveda — three short texts that between them established much of the conceptual vocabulary later Hinduism would use to speak of mind, breath, sound, and liberation.
