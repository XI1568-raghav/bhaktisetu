**Agni** (अग्नि) is the second most-invoked deity in the Rigveda after Indra, with roughly two hundred hymns devoted to him. His significance, however, exceeds even that count, because Agni is present in every Vedic ritual whether or not he is the named addressee — the sacrificial fire is the medium through which all offerings reach the gods. Cognate with Latin *ignis* and Slavic *ogon*, the name Agni itself preserves a Proto-Indo-European root.

## The Opening of the Rigveda

The first verse of the entire Vedic corpus is addressed to Agni:

अग्निमीळे पुरोहितं यज्ञस्य देवमृत्विजम् ।
होतारं रत्नधातमम् ॥
*agnim īḷe purohitaṃ yajñasya devam ṛtvijam*
*hotāraṃ ratnadhātamam*

"I invoke Agni — the household priest, the divine officiant of the sacrifice, the *hotṛ*, the best bestower of treasures."

Three priestly titles are stacked in two lines. Agni is *purohita* (the chaplain who stands before), *ṛtvij* (the season-priest who performs the rites at the right time), and *hotṛ* (the invoker who calls the gods). The hymn announces from the outset that the Vedic sacrifice will be conducted by Agni himself.

## The Divine Priest

Agni's distinctive theological role is that of mediator. He is described as the priest of the gods and the god of the priests — *devānāṃ ca hotā* and *manuṣyāṇāṃ ca devaḥ*. When the human *hotṛ* pours ghee on the fire, Agni carries the offering upward in smoke to the deities. The fire is therefore not merely a tool of worship but the living embodiment of the rite. Without Agni, there is no sacrifice.

## Threefold and Manifold

Vedic theology dwells on Agni's multiple forms. He is **threefold** — terrestrial fire on the altar, atmospheric lightning in the storm, and celestial sun in the sky. Other hymns enumerate his birth in many places: in the *araṇis* (the fire-sticks rubbed together at every kindling), in plants, in waters (where he hides as *Apām Napāt*, the "child of the waters"), in animals, in the dawn, in the sun. Hymn 10.51 narrates how the gods sought the hidden Agni in fear, finally finding him concealed within the waters; his elusiveness gives the Vedic poets some of their most haunting imagery.

## Iconography and Attributes

Agni is described as having two heads, seven tongues (each with a special name — Kālī, Karālī, Manojavā, Sulohitā, Sudhūmravarṇā, Sphuliṅginī, Viśvarūcī), red-gold hair flaming upward, a chariot drawn by red horses or by his own flames, and the wind as his breath. He is "ever young" because he is fresh-born at each kindling, yet "ancient of days" because his lineage is eternal. He is the friend of all (*viśvedevānāṃ sakhā*) and a witness to vows — to this day Hindu marriages are solemnized with seven circumambulations of the fire, with Agni serving as the unwavering witness.

## Domestic and Public Roles

In ritual practice Agni assumes three sacred forms maintained in different fires: the *gārhapatya* (household fire of the householder), the *āhavanīya* (offering fire facing east), and the *dakṣiṇāgni* (southern fire for ancestral rites). These three constitute the **tretāgni**, the three sacred fires whose maintenance was the lifelong duty of the *āhitāgni* Brahmin.

## Continuity into Later Tradition

While Indra's prominence faded in post-Vedic Hinduism, Agni's never did. The fire continues to receive offerings in every Vedic and Smarta ritual — in the daily *agnihotra*, in *havan* and *yajña*, in *upanayana* (sacred-thread investiture), in marriage, and in cremation. The Tamil tradition preserves living *agnihotrin* families to this day. Agni remains the still-burning bridge between the ancient Vedic world and the lived ritual present.
