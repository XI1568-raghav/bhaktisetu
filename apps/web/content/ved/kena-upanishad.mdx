The **Kena Upaniṣad** (केनोपनिषद्) belongs to the Samaveda, attached to the *Talavakāra* (or *Jaiminīya Upaniṣad*) Brāhmaṇa. It takes its name from its opening word — *kena*, "by whom?" — and the question gives the entire short text its character. Across four sections, in alternating verse and prose, the Kena pursues the question of who or what stands behind the senses, the mind, and even the gods themselves.

## The Opening Question

केनेषितं पतति प्रेषितं मनः केन प्राणः प्रथमः प्रैति युक्तः ।
केनेषितां वाचमिमां वदन्ति चक्षुः श्रोत्रं क उ देवो युनक्ति ॥
*keneṣitaṃ patati preṣitaṃ manaḥ kena prāṇaḥ prathamaḥ praiti yuktaḥ*
*keneṣitāṃ vācam imāṃ vadanti cakṣuḥ śrotraṃ ka u devo yunakti*

"By whom commanded, by whom set in motion, does the mind fly forth? By whom does the first breath go on its way? By whom is this speech impelled that people utter? Which god yokes the eye and the ear?"

The disciple does not ask *what* the world is. He asks *who* enables our knowing of it. The Upanishad's project is to point not to the contents of consciousness but to its enabling source.

## The Famous Answer — Negation

The first chapter's reply is among the most carefully phrased in all of Sanskrit philosophy:

यन्मनसा न मनुते येनाहुर्मनो मतम् ।
तदेव ब्रह्म त्वं विद्धि नेदं यदिदमुपासते ॥
*yan manasā na manute yenāhur mano matam*
*tad eva brahma tvaṃ viddhi nedaṃ yad idam upāsate*

"That which the mind does not think but by which, they say, the mind itself is thought — know that alone is *brahman*; not this which people worship."

The pattern is repeated for each faculty. *Brahman* is what the eye does not see but by which sight sees; what the ear does not hear but by which hearing hears; what speech does not utter but by which speech speaks; what breath does not breathe but by which breath is breathed. The Upanishad refuses to make brahman an object of cognition; it is the always-prior subject that makes every cognition possible.

## The Negation of Familiar Worship

The closing phrase — *nedaṃ yad idam upāsate* — "not this which people worship" — is sharp. Whatever the worshipper has fastened on as the object of devotion is, the Kena insists, not yet brahman. Brahman is always the deeper subject behind the chosen object. Shankara, in his commentary, finds in this phrase the entire Advaita critique of all object-bound religion.

## The Allegory of the Yaksha

The third and fourth chapters tell a small mythic story to dramatize the teaching. The gods, having won a great victory over the demons, grew proud. A mysterious *yakṣa* — a luminous spirit — appeared before them. Agni was sent to investigate. "Who are you?" asked the yaksha; Agni boasted, "I am Agni; I can burn anything in the world." The yaksha placed a blade of grass before him; Agni could not burn it. Vāyu came next and could not move it. The gods sent Indra, who reached the yaksha, but the yaksha had vanished — and in his place stood **Umā Haimavatī**, the daughter of Himavān, who told Indra: "It was *brahman*. Through its power you gained your victory."

The story makes two points elegantly. First, even the cosmic powers of the elemental gods are themselves *given* by brahman; they are not self-grounded. Second, it is *Indra* — the chief of the gods — who first comes to the knowledge of brahman, and through the mediating teaching of Umā. The female personification of wisdom appears here in one of her oldest Sanskrit attestations.

## The Closing Teaching

The Upanishad ends with the famous formula on the right approach to brahman:

प्रतिबोधविदितं मतम् ।
*pratibodha-viditaṃ matam*
"It is known to those who awaken to it in every act of awareness."

That is, brahman is not approached as a remote object of inquiry but recognized in the very fact of waking awareness — in the moment when the mind moves to think, when the eye moves to see. The Kena thereby compresses what later Vedanta will call *sākṣi* — the witness consciousness — into a single phrase. The brief final passages on austerity, restraint, and works as the foundation of this knowledge close the text.

The Kena is famous for its severity. It refuses to offer brahman as a comforting object of devotion; it refuses to elaborate cosmogony or psychology. It points, ten ways, to the same unattainable-because-already-attained source, and leaves the listener to recognize it. Its question — *kena?* — is, perhaps, finally also the answer.
