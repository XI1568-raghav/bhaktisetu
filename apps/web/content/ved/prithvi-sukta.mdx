The **Pṛthvī Sūkta** (पृथ्वी सूक्त) is the first hymn of the twelfth *kāṇḍa* of the Atharvaveda Shaunaka recension — sixty-three verses addressed entirely to the Earth herself, who is invoked, praised, and asked to support her child the human worshipper. It is the longest sustained address to a deified Earth anywhere in the Vedic corpus and one of the most ecologically resonant texts in pre-modern world literature.

## Opening Verse

सत्यं बृहदृतमुग्रं दीक्षा तपो ब्रह्म यज्ञः पृथिवीं धारयन्ति ।
सा नो भूतस्य भव्यस्य पत्न्युरुं लोकं पृथिवी नः कृणोतु ॥
*satyaṃ bṛhad ṛtam ugraṃ dīkṣā tapo brahma yajñaḥ pṛthivīṃ dhārayanti*
*sā no bhūtasya bhavyasya patnyuruṃ lokaṃ pṛthivī naḥ kṛṇotu*

"Truth, the great cosmic order, severity, the consecration, ascetic heat, *brahman*, sacrifice — these uphold the Earth. May she, the wife of past and future, make for us a wide world."

The opening immediately situates the Earth not as a passive ground but as a cosmic principle upheld by seven powers — truth, order, ascetic discipline, sacrifice, and the rest. She is *patnī* (wife) of both past and future, suggesting a continuity that binds time itself.

## The Earth Catalogued

The hymn proceeds through what is essentially a poetic geography. The Earth is praised in her every form:

- The Earth on which **mountains stand** and from which the rivers flow.
- The Earth that **bears the seeds** and grants the harvest.
- The Earth that supports **two-footed and four-footed beings** alike.
- The Earth that holds **gold and gems** in her depths.
- The Earth that gives **food, clothing, shelter, and song**.
- The Earth on which the **vedi** (altar) is erected and the *yajña* performed.

Each verse closes with a refrain in the form of a blessing — that the Earth grant strength, that she increase what is good, that she protect from harm. The cumulative effect is of a vast inventory of the natural world, every item of which is acknowledged as a gift.

## The Famous Verse

Among the most often-quoted is the verse on the relation of human and earth:

माता भूमिः पुत्रोऽहं पृथिव्याः ।
*mātā bhūmiḥ putro'haṃ pṛthivyāḥ*
"The Earth is my mother, and I am her son."

This single line has become, in modern Indian environmental rhetoric, the proof-text of an indigenous Hindu ecology. The Indian Constitution's invocation of *bhūmi*, the famous Chipko movement, the contemporary *Bhūmi Pūjā* performed at the start of construction — all reach back, consciously or not, to this verse.

## Asking the Earth's Permission

Several verses ask the Earth's tolerance for what humans must do upon her. The poet acknowledges that we dig her, plough her, build upon her, walk upon her, and finally are buried in her. He prays:

यत् ते भूमे विखनामि क्षिप्रं तदपि रोहतु ।
मा ते मर्म विमृग्वरि मा ते हृदयमर्पिपम् ॥
*yat te bhūme vikhanāmi kṣipraṃ tad api rohatu*
*mā te marma vimṛgvari mā te hṛdayam arpipam*

"O Earth, whatever I dig out of you, may it quickly grow back. O purifier, may I not strike your vital point, may I not pierce your heart."

This is one of the earliest articulations anywhere in world literature of an explicit ethic of restoration — the acknowledgment that human use of the Earth incurs an obligation to its repair.

## The Cultural Vision

The hymn celebrates the cultural plurality of the human Earth. It speaks of the Earth as a place of *jana* (peoples) with different speech, different customs, different rites; the Earth is described as *bahuvidhāṃ* — "of many forms" — *vacasāṃ* — "of many speeches." There is no implication of a single proper culture upon the soil; the Earth is honored precisely because she sustains a multiplicity.

## Contemporary Resonance

The Prithvi Sukta has been adopted in modern times as something like an environmental anthem of Hindu tradition. Verses from it are recited at *Vana Mahotsav* tree-planting ceremonies, at the Earth Day observances of Hindu temples worldwide, at the inauguration of organic-farming initiatives. The 12.1 hymn was also quoted by India at the 1992 Rio Earth Summit. Whether the ancient Atharvavedic poet would recognize this latter-day use is uncertain, but the hymn's central insight — that the Earth is a living mother to whom we owe acknowledgement, restraint, and gratitude — translates across three millennia with remarkable directness.
