The **Puruṣa Sūkta** (पुरुष सूक्त) is Rigveda 10.90, a sixteen-verse hymn that has had perhaps the deepest influence of any single Vedic composition on the religious, social, and philosophical imagination of India. Its central image — the cosmos arising from the sacrificial dismemberment of a primordial Person — is at once mythic, ritual, and metaphysical.

## Opening Verse

सहस्रशीर्षा पुरुषः सहस्राक्षः सहस्रपात् ।
*sahasraśīrṣā puruṣaḥ sahasrākṣaḥ sahasrapāt*
"The Person has a thousand heads, a thousand eyes, a thousand feet."

The hymn opens with a deliberately overwhelming image. *Puruṣa* (पुरुष) — literally "person" or "man" — is here not a finite being but the all-encompassing cosmic principle. He pervades the earth on every side and extends beyond it by "ten fingers," a phrase commentators read as signifying the transcendent surplus of being over manifestation.

## The Cosmic Sacrifice

The structural heart of the hymn (verses 6–10) describes a strange and paradoxical rite: the gods themselves perform a sacrifice in which Puruṣa is both the offerer and the offering. From this self-immolation the manifest world unfolds. The seasons become the implements of the rite; spring is the *ghee*, summer the kindling, autumn the oblation. From the residue of the sacrifice arise the animals, the meters of poetry, the mantras, and the gods Indra and Agni themselves.

## The Origin of the Four Varnas

Verse 12 is among the most cited and most controversial in all Sanskrit literature:

ब्राह्मणोऽस्य मुखमासीद् बाहू राजन्यः कृतः ।
ऊरू तदस्य यद्वैश्यः पद्भ्यां शूद्रो अजायत ॥
*brāhmaṇo'sya mukham āsīd bāhū rājanyaḥ kṛtaḥ*
*ūrū tadasya yad vaiśyaḥ padbhyāṃ śūdro ajāyata*

"The Brahmin was his mouth, the Rajanya [Kshatriya] was made his arms; his thighs became the Vaishya, from his feet the Shudra was born."

This is the locus classicus for the **varṇa** (वर्ण) scheme — the four-fold social ordering of brahmana (priest), kshatriya (warrior-ruler), vaishya (producer-trader), and shudra (server). Scholars note that this is the *only* mention of the four varnas in the Rigveda Samhita, and that Mandala 10 is one of the latest layers. The verse functions less as a record of existing institutions than as a mythic charter for an emerging social order whose elaboration belongs largely to the later Brahmana and Dharmashastra literature.

## Cosmological Continuation

The hymn proceeds to describe how other features of the cosmos emerged from Puruṣa's body: the moon from his mind, the sun from his eye, Indra and Agni from his mouth, the wind from his breath, the sky from his head, the earth from his feet, the regions of space from his ears. The vision is one of radical correspondence: macrocosm and microcosm, body and world, are isomorphic.

## Ritual and Liturgical Use

The Purusha Sukta is recited in many living rites. It appears in the daily *sandhyāvandanam* of some traditions, in temple *abhiṣeka* ceremonies, in *yajña* invocations, and as part of the Vishnu Sahasranama recitations. The hymn is found, with minor variations, across all four Vedas — in the Yajurveda's *Vājasaneyi Saṃhitā* (chapter 31), the Sama, and the Atharva — testifying to its central liturgical importance.

## Interpretive Layers

Vedantic commentators — Shankara above all — read the Puruṣa Sukta as a pointer to the non-dual *brahman*. The "dismemberment" is interpreted as the illusory manifoldness (*māyā*) of the One. Vaishnava commentators identify Puruṣa with Narayana. Modern social reformers, including B. R. Ambedkar, scrutinized verse 12 critically as the ideological root of caste hierarchy. The hymn thus continues to be both adored as scripture and contested as social history — a sign of its enduring power.
