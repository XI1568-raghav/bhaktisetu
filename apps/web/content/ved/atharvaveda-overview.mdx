The **Atharvaveda** (अथर्ववेद) is the fourth and most distinctive of the Vedas. Where the other three Samhitas focus on the high public *yajña* — the great Soma sacrifices, the kindling of the sacred fires, the cosmic praises of Indra and Agni — the Atharvaveda turns toward the more immediate concerns of household and village life: healing illness, ensuring safe childbirth, blessing crops, protecting cattle, removing rivals, securing love and victory. For this reason it was sometimes excluded from older lists of "the three Vedas" (*trayī vidyā*), only to be admitted as a full member of the canon by the late Vedic period.

## Names and Composition

The Veda is named for two of its principal Rishi lineages: **Atharvan** (अथर्वन्), the priest of the auspicious fire, and **Aṅgiras** (अङ्गिरस्), often associated with hymns of more dangerous or aggressive power. An older title preserved in the tradition is therefore **Atharvāṅgirasa Veda**. A third lineage often invoked is the **Bhṛgus**, and several hymns are attributed to the Vasishthas, the Kashyapas, and other Rishis familiar from the Rigveda. The text was composed somewhat later than the Rigveda — most scholars date the main body to roughly 1200–1000 BCE — though it preserves what may be very ancient ritual and folk material.

## Two Recensions

The Atharvaveda survives in two main recensions, structurally rather different:

- The **Śaunaka** recension — the dominant version, comprising 20 *kāṇḍas* (books), 731 hymns, and approximately 6,000 verses. This is the recension printed in standard editions (the great German edition of Roth-Whitney, 1856) and the basis of W. D. Whitney's English translation.
- The **Paippalāda** recension — preserved in a single manuscript discovered in Kashmir in the late nineteenth century and in a separate living tradition among the Atharvavedins of Odisha. Roughly one-eighth larger than the Shaunaka, with substantial textual variation, the Paippalada has been the focus of major recent editorial work by scholars including Michael Witzel, Arlo Griffiths, and Alexander Lubotsky.

## What the Atharvaveda Contains

The hymns fall into several broad categories:

- **Bhaiṣajya** (भैषज्य, "healing") — hymns to cure fever, jaundice, leprosy, snakebite, wounds, mental illness, and many other afflictions, often paired with the use of medicinal herbs.
- **Āyuṣya** — hymns for long life and protection of children.
- **Paustika** — hymns for prosperity, good harvest, success in trade, fertility of cattle.
- **Strīkarmāṇi** — hymns for marriage, conception, safe childbirth, love.
- **Rājakarmāṇi** — hymns of statecraft, royal consecration, military success.
- **Abhicāra** — hymns of aggressive ritual aimed at enemies, including counter-sorcery.
- **Prāyaścitta** — hymns for atonement and the removal of sin.
- **Philosophical hymns** — including the **Pṛthvī Sūkta** (12.1), the **Kāla** (Time) hymns of book 19, the **Skambha** (Cosmic Pillar) hymns of book 10, and the **Brahmacārin** hymn (11.5) — speculative compositions that anticipate the Upanishads.

## The Adjustment of Status

Because much of its content addressed practical or "magical" concerns, the Atharvaveda was treated with some reserve by the Vedic priesthood. The fourth main priest of the great sacrifices, the **brahman**, is in the developed *śrauta* tradition the priest of the Atharvaveda — but the inclusion came late, and some Brahmana texts still speak of "the three Vedas" without it. By the time of the late Upanishads and the Dharmashastra literature, however, the Atharvaveda is fully accepted, and several of the most important Upanishads — *Muṇḍaka*, *Praśna*, *Māṇḍūkya* — are affiliated with it.

## Significance

The Atharvaveda is the indispensable Vedic window onto everyday ancient Indian life. Where the Rigveda preserves the world-view of warrior aristocrats and priestly poets, the Atharvaveda preserves the concerns of farmers, mothers, healers, soldiers, traders, and householders. It records the first detailed catalogue of medicinal herbs in the subcontinent (an important precursor to Ayurveda), the earliest descriptions of household rites still performed today, and the prayers of people facing the ordinary fears — disease, jealous rivals, difficult births, snakes in the field — that shape religion as much as cosmic speculation does. To omit the Atharvaveda is to read the Vedic period with one eye closed.
