The Rigveda contains 1,028 hymns, but a handful have shaped Hindu thought, ritual, and culture out of all proportion to their number. The following short canon represents the hymns most often cited, recited, and studied today.

## The Gāyatrī Mantra (RV 3.62.10)

Composed by Viśvāmitra in the *gāyatrī* meter (24 syllables, hence the name), this verse is the most-recited mantra in the entire Vedic corpus.

तत्सवितुर्वरेण्यं भर्गो देवस्य धीमहि ।
धियो यो नः प्रचोदयात् ॥
*tat savitur vareṇyaṃ bhargo devasya dhīmahi*
*dhiyo yo naḥ pracodayāt*

"May we meditate on the desirable splendor of the god Savitṛ; may he inspire our thoughts."

Whispered at dawn and dusk by initiated Brahmins for more than two and a half millennia, the Gayatri Mantra is the heart of the daily *sandhyāvandanam* and the seed-mantra of the *upanayana* sacred-thread ceremony.

## The Purusha Sukta (RV 10.90)

Sixteen verses on cosmic creation as the dismemberment of a primordial Person, sung in every major Vaishnava temple liturgy and the *locus classicus* for the *varṇa* scheme (see the dedicated article).

## The Nāsadīya Sukta (RV 10.129)

Seven verses of meditative inquiry into cosmic origin, ending with the celebrated agnostic line *"he surely knows, or perhaps he knows not."* The hymn is foundational for later Vedantic theology and a touchstone for Indian philosophical humility.

## The Hiraṇyagarbha Sukta (RV 10.121)

The hymn of the "Golden Womb," each verse closing with *kasmai devāya haviṣā vidhema* — "to which god shall we offer?" — and resolved finally with the naming of Prajapati.

## The Devī Sukta (RV 10.125)

Eight verses spoken in the first person by **Vāc**, the goddess of speech, daughter of the sage Ambhṛṇa. The opening line declares:

अहं रुद्रेभिर्वसुभिश्चराम्यहमादित्यैरुत विश्वदेवैः ।
*ahaṃ rudrebhir vasubhiś carāmy aham ādityair uta viśvadevaiḥ*

"I move with the Rudras and Vasus, with the Adityas and the All-Gods."

This is the foundational text of *Śākta* theology — the goddess as the speaking subject of the cosmos. The hymn forms the conclusion of the daily recitation of the *Devī Māhātmya*.

## The Mahāmṛtyuñjaya Mantra (RV 7.59.12)

A three-line invocation of Tryambaka (Rudra) for healing and protection from death, composed by Vasishtha.

त्र्यम्बकं यजामहे सुगन्धिं पुष्टिवर्धनम् ।
उर्वारुकमिव बन्धनान्मृत्योर्मुक्षीय मामृतात् ॥
*tryambakaṃ yajāmahe sugandhiṃ puṣṭivardhanam*
*urvārukam iva bandhanān mṛtyor mukṣīya māmṛtāt*

"We worship the three-eyed one, fragrant, the nourisher. As the ripe cucumber falls from its stem, may I be freed from death — not from immortality."

Recited daily in Shaiva worship and at moments of illness or transition.

## The Frog Hymn (RV 7.103)

A wry seven-verse comparison of Brahmin priests reciting in chorus to frogs croaking together after the first monsoon rains. Modern Indologists read it variously as gentle self-satire, as a fertility hymn celebrating the monsoon, and as evidence of the early Vedic poets' capacity for humor.

## The Funeral Hymn (RV 10.18)

The principal Vedic hymn for cremation rites, addressing Mrityu (Death), the departed soul, and the surviving relatives in turn. Its imagery of the *pitṛs* — the ancestors who have crossed before — underlies the entire later *śrāddha* tradition.

## The Wedding Hymn (RV 10.85) — Sūryā-vivāha

The mythic wedding of Sūryā (the sun's daughter) to Soma, supplying the verses still used in Hindu marriage ceremonies, including the seven-step *saptapadī*.

## The River Hymn (RV 10.75) — Nadī Stuti

Praise of the rivers of the Vedic homeland — the Sindhu, Sarasvati, Yamuna, Ganga, and others — providing the most precise geographical anchor available for the Rigvedic milieu.

## A Living Selection

This list could be extended. The *Aprī* hymns of ritual welcome, the *Vṛṣākapi* hymn of mythic peculiarity, the dialogue of *Yamī and Yama*, the cattle-finding hymn of Saramā — each has its own readers and its own ritual life. To enter the Rigveda is to enter a forest of voices; these few are the most familiar paths through it.
