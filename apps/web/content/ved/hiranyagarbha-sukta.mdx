The **Hiraṇyagarbha Sūkta** (हिरण्यगर्भ सूक्त), Rigveda 10.121, is a hymn of ten verses devoted to the cosmic principle that emerged at the dawn of creation as a luminous "golden womb." Less philosophically severe than the *Nāsadīya*, less mythologically dense than the *Puruṣa*, the Hiraṇyagarbha hymn occupies a middle ground in which a definite creator is invoked even as the seeker's question — *which god shall we worship?* — refuses to settle on a single name.

## Opening Verse

हिरण्यगर्भः समवर्तताग्रे भूतस्य जातः पतिरेक आसीत् ।
स दाधार पृथिवीं द्यामुतेमां कस्मै देवाय हविषा विधेम ॥
*hiraṇyagarbhaḥ samavartatāgre bhūtasya jātaḥ patir eka āsīt*
*sa dādhāra pṛthivīṃ dyām utemāṃ kasmai devāya haviṣā vidhema*

"In the beginning the Golden Womb arose, born the one lord of all that exists. He upheld the earth and yonder sky — to which god shall we offer with oblation?"

The image of a **hiraṇyagarbha** — literally "golden embryo" or "golden womb" — is at once feminine (womb) and masculine (the lord born from it). The verse compresses a complete cosmogony: an undifferentiated golden potential first appears, and from it emerges the single sovereign who supports earth and heaven.

## The Refrain

Every verse but the last closes with the same question:

कस्मै देवाय हविषा विधेम
*kasmai devāya haviṣā vidhema*
"To which god shall we offer with our oblation?"

Indian tradition, beginning with the Brahmana literature, took the interrogative pronoun *ka* (कः, "who") as itself the name of the creator — **Ka** — and identified this Ka with **Prajāpati** (प्रजापति), the "Lord of Creatures." The grammatical accident becomes a theological insight: the answer to *which god?* is the god whose proper name is the question.

## The Cosmic Functions

Verses 2 through 9 catalog the attributes of this supreme being:

- He gives life and strength; gods themselves obey him.
- His shadow is immortality, his shadow death.
- He surveys the snow-covered mountains, the world ocean, the heavenly waters.
- He fixed the firmament; he found the wide air.
- He measured the regions of space.
- The waters carried the universal embryo, in which all the gods came together.

The catalog is essentially a job description for monotheism, sketched in Vedic vocabulary. The hymn marks a clear movement away from the polytheism of the earlier Mandalas toward a single supreme principle — a movement that the late Rigveda exhibits repeatedly.

## The Concluding Verse

The final, tenth verse — likely a later addition — finally names the deity:

प्रजापते न त्वदेतान्यन्यो विश्वा जातानि परि ता बभूव ।
*prajāpate na tvad etāny anyo viśvā jātāni pari tā babhūva*

"O Prajapati, no other than you encompasses all these creatures that have come into being."

With this verse the open question of the refrain is closed: Ka is Prajapati. Scholars treat the addition as a sign of the hymn's reception in the late Vedic and Brahmana period, when Prajapati had become the principal creator deity in ritual theology.

## Later Reception

In Vedantic literature Hiraṇyagarbha is identified with the subtle cosmic body — the totality of all individual subtle bodies — and equated with **Brahmā** as the creator. In yoga literature the term denotes a stage of consciousness in which the seer perceives the unified source of phenomena. The hymn is recited in Vaishnava temples as part of *Viṣṇu Sahasranāma* preliminaries and in *havan* rites across the Hindu world.

The Hiraṇyagarbha Sūkta thus stands between the unresolved questioning of the Nasadiya and the ordered ritualism of the later Brahmanas — a hymn in which Vedic religion is reaching, with the hesitation of a question, for the One.
