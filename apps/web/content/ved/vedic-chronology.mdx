Few questions in Indology have generated as much discussion as the dating of the Vedas. The texts themselves contain no calendar dates; their composers were oral poets, not chroniclers. All chronology must therefore be inferred from internal linguistic evidence, comparison with Indo-European cognate languages, references in later literature, and — more recently — archaeology and genetics. The scholarly consensus has shifted considerably since the nineteenth century, and significant uncertainty remains.

## Müller's Conjectural Dating

Friedrich Max Müller, working in the 1850s, was among the first Western scholars to propose a chronology for the Vedic period. Reasoning backward from the late Vedic period (which he tied to the Buddha, then dated around 500 BCE) and allowing four to five centuries for each textual layer, he placed the composition of the Rigveda around 1200 BCE. Müller himself acknowledged the figure was a minimum estimate; he wrote that the Vedas might be considerably older, but no rigorous method then existed to push earlier dates.

## The Modern Philological Consensus

Twentieth- and twenty-first-century Vedic scholarship, exemplified by the work of Michael Witzel, Stanley Insler, and Asko Parpola, has refined Müller's framework using comparative linguistics and stratigraphic analysis of the texts themselves. The current mainstream view holds:

- **Rigveda Samhita** — composed c. 1500–1200 BCE, with the family books (Maṇḍalas 2–7) likely the oldest core.
- **Atharvaveda, Sama, and Yajur Samhitas** — c. 1200–900 BCE.
- **Brahmana texts** — c. 900–700 BCE.
- **Early Upanishads** (Brihadaranyaka, Chandogya) — c. 700–500 BCE.
- **Later Upanishads** — c. 500 BCE – 200 CE.

This chronology aligns with the diffusion of Indo-Aryan speakers into the northwestern subcontinent following the decline of the Indus Valley Civilization around 1900 BCE.

## Evidence and Methods

Several converging strands support the philological dating:

- **Comparative linguistics.** Vedic Sanskrit shares features with Avestan (Old Iranian) and other Indo-European languages, placing it within an attested family tree whose internal chronology can be calibrated.
- **Material culture references.** The Rigveda mentions horses, spoked chariots, and bronze metallurgy — technologies that appear archaeologically in the relevant region in the second millennium BCE.
- **River geography.** Hymns reference the Saraswati as a great river. Geological work suggests the Ghaggar-Hakra system carried more water in the early-to-mid second millennium BCE, narrowing a plausible composition window.
- **Astronomical allusions.** Sparse references to nakshatra positions allow tentative dating, though such allusions are also the most contested form of evidence.

## Alternative Views

A range of Indian scholars — including B. G. Tilak in the nineteenth century and more recently Subhash Kak, David Frawley, and proponents of the "Indigenous Aryans" thesis — argue for substantially earlier dates, sometimes placing the Rigveda in the fourth or fifth millennium BCE. They draw on archaeoastronomical readings (such as the *Vernal Equinox in Orion* argument), connections to Indus Valley iconography, and recent ancient-DNA studies. Mainstream Indology generally finds these arguments methodologically weak — astronomical readings are highly underdetermined, and genetic evidence published in the 2018–2019 Rakhigarhi and Narasimhan et al. studies remains consistent with steppe-pastoralist migration in the second millennium BCE.

## Conclusion

The dating debate is unlikely to be definitively settled. What is reasonably secure is the relative chronology of the layers — Samhitas before Brahmanas before Upanishads — and the broad placement of the corpus within the Late Bronze Age and Early Iron Age. Beyond that, scholarly humility is appropriate: the Vedas are, as Witzel writes, "tape recordings" from a time before tape, and our calibration of the recording date remains imperfect.
