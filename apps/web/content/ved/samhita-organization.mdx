The word **saṃhitā** (संहिता) — literally "collection" or "joined together" — denotes the most foundational layer of any Vedic text: the gathered body of mantras themselves, as distinct from the prose commentaries (Brahmanas), the forest treatises (Aranyakas), and the philosophical dialogues (Upanishads) that grew around them. Each of the four Vedas has its own Samhita, and each Samhita is organized by its own internal scheme. Understanding these schemes is essential for navigating Vedic literature.

## The Rigveda Samhita

The Rigveda's organizing units, in descending order of size, are:

- **Maṇḍala** (मण्डल, "circle" or "book") — 10 in total.
- **Anuvāka** (अनुवाक, "lesson") — variable number per Mandala.
- **Sūkta** (सूक्त, "hymn") — 1,028 in total.
- **Ṛc** or **Mantra** — 10,552 in total, the individual verses.

A typical citation in modern scholarship is therefore by Mandala, Sukta, and Mantra — for example, RV 3.62.10 (the Gayatri Mantra) means Mandala 3, Sukta 62, Verse 10.

The Rigveda also preserves an older organizational system, the **aṣṭaka** division. The entire text is divided into 8 *aṣṭakas* (sections), each of 8 *adhyāyas* (chapters), each of *vargas* (groups of about five mantras). This system was purely a mechanical division for memorization in equal portions and bears no relation to thematic content. Older manuscripts and many printed editions show both numbering systems in parallel.

## The Yajurveda Samhitas

The Yajurveda has two main forms with significantly different organization:

### Shukla Yajurveda — Vājasaneyi Samhita

Composed in 40 **adhyāyas** (chapters). Adhyaya 40 is the famous **Īśāvāsya Upaniṣad** — the only Upanishad that forms part of a Samhita rather than being attached to a Brahmana or Aranyaka. The Vājasaneyi Samhita is *pure mantra*: the explanatory passages have all been moved to the separately preserved Shatapatha Brahmana.

### Krishna Yajurveda — Taittirīya Samhita

Composed in 7 **kāṇḍas** (books), each of multiple *prapāṭhakas*, each of *anuvākas*, each of mantras. Unlike the Shukla recension the Taittirīya interleaves mantra and Brahmana commentary, so the Samhita and its explanatory prose form a continuous reading.

## The Samaveda Samhita

The Samaveda has two principal divisions:

- The **Pūrvārcika** (पूर्वार्चिक, "first collection") — about 650 verses, arranged by deity.
- The **Uttarārcika** (उत्तरार्चिक, "later collection") — about 1,225 verses, arranged by liturgical function.

Within each section the units are *daśats* (groups of ten) and individual *ṛcs*. Citations typically reference the Pūrvārcika or Uttarārcika, the *prapāṭhaka*, the *daśat*, and the verse.

## The Atharvaveda Samhita

The Atharvaveda is organized into 20 **kāṇḍas** (books) divided into *sūktas* and *mantras*. The Shaunaka recension has 731 hymns containing approximately 6,000 verses. Book 19, sometimes considered a later addition, contains a particular concentration of philosophical hymns including the *Kāla* (Time) suktas. The Paippalāda recension has a similar but not identical organization.

## Samhita-patha vs Pada-patha

A critical distinction in the actual textual transmission of any Samhita is between two parallel forms:

- **Saṃhitā-pāṭha** — the continuous text, with the rules of *sandhi* (euphonic combination) fully applied between words. This is how the Veda is recited in normal flowing speech.
- **Pada-pāṭha** — the same text with each word separated and *sandhi* resolved. Hidden compounds are analyzed; verb forms are spelled out without combining with adjacent particles.

The Pada-patha is itself a separate scholarly work, traditionally attributed to **Śākalya** for the Rigveda (probably around the 6th century BCE) and to other ancient grammarians for the other Vedas. The Pada-patha is invaluable for scholarly purposes — it makes morphology explicit — but the *primary* form of the Veda for ritual recitation is always the Samhita-patha.

A common manuscript layout presents the Samhita-patha and the Pada-patha in parallel, often with the accent marks inscribed above and below the syllables. A trained reciter can move between the two with fluency.

## The Khila Hymns

In addition to the main body of each Samhita, some Vedic schools preserve a small number of **khila** (खिल, "supplementary") hymns — hymns whose attestation is less secure or whose acceptance into the canonical Samhita was disputed. The Rigveda Shakala recension preserves about 36 khilas in some manuscripts. The famous **Śrī Sūkta** — the hymn to the goddess Lakshmi widely recited today — is a Rigvedic khila rather than a hymn of the main Samhita.

## Why Organization Matters

The Samhita organization is not just bibliographic. It encodes a memorization plan, a priestly division of labor, and an internal hierarchy of importance. Knowing that a verse is Rigveda 1.1.1 (the opening hymn to Agni, first verse of the first Sukta of the first Mandala) is to know that one is hearing the Vedic text's *first words* — the inaugural verse with which all study traditionally begins. Knowing that the Isha Upanishad is *adhyāya* 40 of the Vājasaneyi Samhita is to know that it stands at the very end of the Yajurvedic mantra collection, in a position of programmatic finality. The organization is itself the first commentary the tradition offers on its own scripture.
