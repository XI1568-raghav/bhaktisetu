The word **veda** (वेद, *veda*) derives from the Sanskrit root √*vid*, meaning "to know." The Vedas are therefore literally "knowledge texts," and within Hindu tradition they are regarded as the most ancient and authoritative body of sacred literature. Composed in Vedic Sanskrit over roughly a millennium, the corpus is traditionally classified as **śruti** (शृति) — "that which is heard" — meaning eternal revelation received by the ancient seers (*ṛṣis*) rather than authored by human beings.

## The Four Vedas

The canonical Vedic corpus is divided into four collections:

- **Rigveda** (ऋग्वेद, *Ṛgveda*) — hymns of praise to the gods, the oldest layer.
- **Yajurveda** (यजुर्वेद, *Yajurveda*) — sacrificial formulae used by the *adhvaryu* priest.
- **Samaveda** (सामवेद, *Sāmaveda*) — melodic chants drawn largely from the Rigveda.
- **Atharvaveda** (अथर्ववेद, *Atharvaveda*) — hymns and incantations addressing daily life, healing, and statecraft.

Each Veda is itself stratified into four textual layers: the **Samhita** (mantra collection), the **Brahmana** (ritual prose commentary), the **Aranyaka** (forest treatise of contemplative ritual), and the **Upanishad** (philosophical dialogue). Together these layers move from outward worship to inward inquiry, culminating in the metaphysical speculation called **Vedanta** — literally "the end of the Vedas."

## Composition and Preservation

The Rigveda Samhita, the oldest stratum, is generally dated by Indologists between roughly 1500 and 1200 BCE, with parts of the family books (Mandalas 2–7) possibly older. The remaining Samhitas, Brahmanas, and early Upanishads were composed in stages down to about 500 BCE. The texts were preserved orally with extraordinary precision through *pāṭha* recitation schemes — *padapāṭha*, *kramapāṭha*, *jaṭā*, and *ghana* — that interleave words so that errors become immediately audible. UNESCO recognized the tradition of Vedic chanting as a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity in 2003.

## Religious and Philosophical Significance

The Vedas occupy a unique position in Hindu thought. They are considered *apauruṣeya* (अपौरुषेय) — not of human authorship — and serve as the ultimate *pramāṇa* (means of valid knowledge) for orthodox (*āstika*) schools such as Mimamsa and Vedanta. Heterodox movements like Buddhism and Jainism rejected Vedic authority, defining themselves in part by that rejection. The early Vedic worldview emphasizes *ṛta* (cosmic order), *yajña* (sacrifice), and a pantheon of devas including Indra, Agni, Varuna, Mitra, Soma, and the Ashvins. Later layers progressively turn from ritual outwardness to inquiry into *brahman*, *ātman*, and liberation (*mokṣa*).

## A Living Tradition

Despite their antiquity the Vedas remain in living use. Trained *adhvaryu*, *hotṛ*, *udgātṛ*, and *brahman* priests still officiate at *agnihotra* and *somayāga* rituals, particularly in Kerala, Karnataka, Maharashtra, and Tamil Nadu. *Veda pāṭhaśālās* — traditional schools of recitation — continue to train new generations in the precise pronunciation and accentuation (*svara*) that the oral lineage demands. For contemporary Hindus the Vedas function simultaneously as scripture, as cultural memory, and as the wellspring from which later Sanskrit literature, philosophy, and ritual all flow.
