# How to Send Prasad Online to India (Without the Sanctity Lost in Shipping)

You are in Edison or Leicester, your mother's *tithi* is Tuesday, and you cannot fly home. The family priest has the *shraadh* handled, but the small thing — the *prasad* you would have brought to your in-laws' door — is the part with your name on it. That is the moment most NRIs first try to *send prasad online* to India, and the question gets sharp: is this actually *prasad*, or a sweet box from a warehouse with a tulsi sticker on top? The honest answer: yes, if it has been offered in a real *puja* before it ships; no, if not. This guide is for devotees abroad sending *prasad* to family in India for festivals, *tithis*, and life events — and for India-based devotees gifting across cities. We cover what *prasad* is, where online services fail, and how [Darshya](/about) handles it.

## What "prasad" actually is

The word *prasad* (Sanskrit *prasāda*, "grace") is often translated as "blessed sweets," which is true but incomplete. The Bhagavad Gita grounds the practice in the offering of food to the divine first; returning that offering as *prasad* is what sanctifies it. The category is broader than ladoos.

A short taxonomy of what gets called *prasad*:

- **Temple *prasad*** — what the temple distributes after *aarti* or *darshan*. Tirupati: the ladoo. Jagannath Puri: *mahaprasad*. Vaishno Devi: dry items (mishri, *raksha-sutra*, *kumkum*). Many South Indian temples include *theertham* (sanctified water) and *vibhuti* (sacred ash).
- **Puja *prasad*** — items offered during a specific ritual: *panchamrit* residue from an *abhishek* (milk, curd, ghee, honey, sugar), Tulsi leaves from the *bhog*, *kumkum* and *akshat* (turmeric-rice) from the *sankalp* tray, the *raksha-sutra* tied at the close.
- ***Naivedyam* / *bhog*** — food cooked and offered to the deity during the puja. Once offered, it becomes *prasad*. *Naivedyam* in the South, *bhog* in the North; same idea.
- **Sanctified objects** — a *yantra*, a *rudraksha*, a *chunari* offered at the deity, a coconut from the *havan*.

The defining property is not what the item is. It is that it has passed through worship. A box of identical sweets from the same halwai becomes *prasad* if and only if it was offered first. Otherwise it is a sweet box.

## Why "online prasad" fails when it fails

Most disappointment with online *prasad* services traces to three failure modes. We name them because most competitor pages will not.

**One: the *prasad* is packed before any *puja* happens.** The most common fraud. A vendor packs sweet boxes from a kitchen in Noida or Saidapet and ships them as "*prasad* from [famous temple]." There was no offering. The label is decorative.

**Two: the *prasad* ships from a warehouse, not a temple.** A milder version of the first. The puja may have happened, but the shipped stock came from a fulfilment centre. Tell-tale: the same SKU sold by twenty unrelated "temple *prasad*" sites — identical packaging, identical photo.

**Three: the *prasad* arrives stale, broken, or three weeks late.** A perishable mawa-sweet ships without cold chain and arrives inedible. A clay diya cracks. A festival deadline passes while the parcel sits in customs.

A trustworthy service has to clear all three. One out of three is not enough.

## What to look for in a trustworthy service

A devotee's checklist before placing an order:

- **Verifiable temple source.** A named temple, a real address, ideally a verification badge. Generic "blessed at a Himalayan temple" copy is a red flag.
- **Named priest or pujari.** The person who performed the *puja* should be identifiable. If the site will not name them, the offering may not have happened.
- **Photo or video proof of preparation.** A still of the *prasad* on the *vedi*, or — better — a clip of the priest performing the *naivedyam* and setting aside what ships to you.
- **Traceable booking number.** A unique per-order reference, not a generic invoice. On Darshya this is the `BS-YYYYMMDD-XXXXX` booking ID, the same format used for puja bookings (see the [Kashi darshan post](/blog/kashi-vishwanath-live-darshan) for how this works on the puja side).
- **Clear shipping timelines, in writing.** India domestic and international quoted separately. Festival deadlines stated, not implied.
- **Refund policy if delivery fails.** What happens if the parcel does not arrive, arrives broken, or arrives after the festival.
- **Honest disclosure of what cannot be shipped.** A vendor who promises fresh *panchamrit* to New Jersey is either freezing it (rare) or lying (common).

## Festival and life-event prasad guide

Different occasions call for different *prasad*. A traditionally grounded list:

| Occasion | Customary *prasad* | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| *Akshaya Tritiya* | Yellow sweets, *til-laddoo*, gold-coloured *boondi* | Day of imperishable merit |
| Diwali / Lakshmi Puja | *Mathri*, *mawa* sweets, *kheel-batashe*, dry fruit | Dry items travel better |
| *Janmashtami* | *Makhan-mishri*, *panjiri*, *dhania-panjiri* | *Panjiri* keeps for weeks; *makhan* does not ship |
| Ganesh Chaturthi | *Modak* (steamed or fried), coconut-jaggery sweets | Fried *modak* travels; steamed does not |
| *Shraadh* / *tithi* | *Anna* (rice), *til*, *kheer* — NOT festive sweets | The most-misordered category; do not send Diwali mithai for a *shraadh* |
| Holi | Gujiya, *thandai* spice mix (dry), dry colour | Wet colour and *bhang* are not *prasad* |
| Navratri (vrat) | *Sabudana*, *kuttu*, *singhare*, fruit, *makhana* | Must be *vrat-compliant* |
| Birthday | Temple-specific or deity-favourite item | Tirupati: ladoo. ISKCON: *Govinda's* sweets. Pick the family's deity |
| Wedding anniversary | Deity-specific, often a *sankalp* with both *gotras* | *Raksha-sutra* and *kumkum* often included |
| Ganga Dussehra | *Tirtha-prasad* — sealed Ganga water plus *naivedyam* | Check destination customs rules |

The rule: festive sweets for festive occasions, austere items for *shraadh* and observances. When in doubt, ask the family priest in India what they would normally serve, and order that.

## International shipping reality check

**India to US, UK, UAE, Canada:** typically 7–14 days door-to-door, sometimes longer through customs. Some destinations restrict dairy and seed-based products; *prasad* generally clears as a low-value gift but not always. Stick to dry, sealed, shelf-stable items: *panjiri*, *til-laddoo*, dry fruit packs, sealed *raksha-sutra* and *akshat* sachets, *vibhuti*, sealed *Ganga jal*. Avoid fresh flowers, milk-based sweets without preservatives, fragile clay diyas, perishable *panchamrit*.

**India domestic, city to city:** typically 2–4 days for major metros, 4–7 days for smaller towns. Fresh sweets travel acceptably in cool months and risk spoilage May to August. For *shraadh* deliveries with a hard date, order at least 5 working days ahead. Festivals on the [Darshya darshan calendar](/darshan) carry an order-by date for prasad shipment.
